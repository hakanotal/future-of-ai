[Derek Parfit](/e/derek-parfit)'s discovery, in Part IV of [Reasons and Persons](/e/reasons-and-persons) (1984), that a few steps each of which seems obviously right lead somewhere almost everyone finds unacceptable.

**The idea.** Start with ten billion people living wonderful lives. Add more people whose lives are good but less so; the world seems no worse, since the new people are glad to exist. Then equalize everyone's welfare a little upward from the new average. Repeat. Eventually you conclude that an enormous population of lives barely worth living is *better* than the flourishing ten billion. Parfit called it repugnant, and spent decades failing to find a clean escape.

**The question it leaves you with.** Is a world better because it has more people in it, if they are all barely content? Your answer determines how much you care about the trillions of possible future people at the heart of [longtermism](/e/longtermism).

**Where it goes in the story.** Longtermist arithmetic counts possible future lives. Any theory that counts more lives as better has to answer Parfit's puzzle. MacAskill engages it in [What We Owe the Future](/e/what-we-owe-the-future), and it is why population ethics, an obscure corner of philosophy, suddenly mattered to billion-dollar funding decisions.

**Strongest objection / best reply.** Some philosophers now argue that we should simply accept the conclusion, and that our intuition misfires at large numbers. Others treat it as proof that "more lives is better" cannot be the whole story. Either way, the move is familiar: logic versus intuition, and someone must decide which gives way.
