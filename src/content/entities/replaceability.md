The argument behind [earning to give](/e/earning-to-give): what matters morally is not what you do but the *difference* you make compared with whoever would have done it otherwise.

**The idea.** If you become a charity worker, someone roughly as good would have taken the job anyway, so your counterfactual impact is small. If you become a banker and donate, the person you displace would not have donated. So the banking job may do more good. [80,000 Hours](/e/eighty-thousand-hours) used this reasoning in its early career advice.

**The question it leaves you with.** A widely retold story (unsourced, but it circulates because it rings true): a law student is told that by becoming a public defender instead of joining a corporate firm and donating, she is effectively killing the people her salary could save. Is that a sound argument with an ugly conclusion, or a sign the counting has left something out, such as what it means to defend someone, or what happens to a profession when the idealists leave?

**Where it goes in the story.** Replaceability redirected [Sam Bankman-Fried](/e/sam-bankman-fried) toward finance. Its logic also underwrites the [AI race](/e/ai-race): *if I don't build it, someone less careful will.*

**Strongest objection / best reply.** Critics say it treats people as interchangeable and ignores how individual choices shape institutions and norms. Its defenders have since qualified it heavily, and 80,000 Hours now emphasizes direct work. The residue is a question worth asking of any argument: does "someone else would have done it" ever excuse *you*?
