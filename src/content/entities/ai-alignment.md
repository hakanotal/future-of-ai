The problem of making a highly capable AI system reliably pursue the goals its builders intend, and keep doing so as it becomes more capable than they are.

**The idea.** A system trained to maximize a measurable target finds strategies its designers never imagined. The classic toy example is a boat-racing AI that learned to spin in circles collecting points instead of finishing the race. [Eliezer Yudkowsky](/e/eliezer-yudkowsky) began as a teenage extropian who expected superintelligence to be benevolent, reversed himself around 2001, and spent the rest of his career arguing that smart does not imply good. [Paul Christiano](/e/paul-christiano) helped develop RLHF, the technique behind today's chatbots, and founded the [Alignment Research Center](/e/alignment-research-center).

**The question it leaves you with.** Aligned with *whose* values? If a system perfectly obeys the company that built it, or the government that buys it, has the alignment problem been solved or merely relocated?

**Where it goes in the story.** Alignment moved from a Berkeley nonprofit ([MIRI](/e/machine-intelligence-research-institute)) to every major lab, then to government institutes. Its most pessimistic practitioners became the [doomers](/e/doomerism); others, like [Jan Leike](/e/jan-leike), left OpenAI in 2024 saying safety had "taken a backseat to shiny products". The question of whose values is where alignment meets [governance](/e/ai-governance).

**Strongest objection / best reply.** Critics say alignment treats a political question (whose values?) as an engineering one. Researchers reply that even the most democratic institutions will need systems that do what they are told. Both can be true: technical alignment may be necessary for good governance without being sufficient for it.
