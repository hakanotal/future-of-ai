The worry that a morality counting only outcomes will license any present wrong, whether lying, fraud or coercion, if the projected future benefit is large enough.

**The idea.** Classical utilitarianism has no intrinsic prohibitions; everything depends on consequences. Most utilitarians add "rules of thumb" (don't lie, don't steal) because breaking them usually backfires. But when the stakes are said to be trillions of future lives, the rule of thumb looks negotiable.

**The question it leaves you with.** Is there anything you would refuse to do even if you were certain it would save a million lives? If yes, where does that line come from, since it can't come from the arithmetic alone? If no, what stops someone who is *wrongly* certain?

**Where it goes in the story.** The [FTX collapse](/e/ftx-collapse) is the test case. [Émile P. Torres](/e/emile-torres) reads Bankman-Fried as having followed the logic: sure, a little fraud, but look at what it funds. [MacAskill](/e/william-macaskill) argues that his actions were contrary to EA's values and better explained by hubris. The same structure appears at larger scale whenever an AI lab justifies speed by the good it will do.

**Strongest objection / best reply.** EA leaders stress that their movement explicitly rejects "naive" utilitarianism and fraud, and Bankman-Fried's own messages ([the Vox DMs](/e/sbf-vox-interview)) suggest cynicism more than conviction. Critics ask whether a framework that must keep warning its members not to follow its logic too far has a design problem.
