[Peter Singer](/e/peter-singer)'s essay, written in Oxford in 1971 and published in *Philosophy & Public Affairs* in 1972, as millions fled famine and war in East Bengal.

It contains the [drowning child](/e/drowning-child) and the principle that follows from it: "if it is in our power to prevent something very bad from happening, without thereby sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, we ought, morally, to do it." Singer draws the radical conclusion openly: the line between duty and charity is in the wrong place, and the comfortable owe far more than they give. Forty years later it became the founding text of [effective altruism](/e/effective-altruism).

**A question to take away:** Singer's example costs you muddy clothes. What is the largest sacrifice you think the argument can ask of you before it stops being convincing, and is that a flaw in the argument or in you?
