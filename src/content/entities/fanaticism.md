The objection that [expected-value](/e/expected-utility) reasoning, followed strictly, lets tiny probabilities of astronomical payoffs override everything else.

**The idea.** Bostrom's own parable, "Pascal's Mugging" (2009): a stranger asks for your wallet and promises, in return, to use powers from another dimension to create an astronomical number of happy lives. You think he is almost certainly lying. But multiply even a one-in-a-trillion chance by a big enough number and the expected value says hand it over.

**The question it leaves you with.** Where exactly is the difference between the mugger and a [longtermist](/e/longtermism) funding pitch that says: this research has a tiny chance of reducing extinction risk, and the future holds [10^24 lives](/e/strong-longtermism-2021)? If you want to refuse the mugger but fund the research, what principle lets you do both?

**Where it goes in the story.** Greaves and MacAskill see the worry coming. Their paper concedes that the case could look "driven purely by tiny credences in highly speculative scenarios" and tests much smaller estimates. Critics say the conclusion survives only because the numbers can always be made big enough.

**Strongest objection / best reply.** Some philosophers bite the bullet and defend fanaticism as the price of consistency. Others propose discounting tiny probabilities, which avoids the mugging but breaks the maths that made longtermism compelling. There is no settled answer, which is itself worth knowing when billions of dollars rest on the calculation.
