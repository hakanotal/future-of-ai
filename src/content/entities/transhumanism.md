The backbone of the bundle: the belief that humans should use technology to overcome biological limits, first disease and ageing, then cognition and emotion, and perhaps finally death and the human form itself.

**The idea.** The word was popularized by the biologist [Julian Huxley](/e/julian-huxley) in [New Bottles for New Wine](/e/new-bottles-for-new-wine) (1957): "the human species can, if it wishes, transcend itself… in its entirety, as humanity." The modern version is concrete and marketed. A brain implant from [Neuralink](/e/neuralink). A cryonics contract with [Alcor](/e/alcor). An essay like Bostrom's [Why I Want to Be a Posthuman When I Grow Up](/e/posthuman-essay-2008), which asks you to imagine healthspan, memory and joy expanded beyond anything you can currently feel.

**The question it leaves you with.** Suppose a pill made you permanently smarter, kinder and healthier, but cost as much as a house. Would you take it? Would you want your neighbour to? And who would be "improved" first?

**Where it goes in the story.** Huxley was also a committed eugenicist, president of the British Eugenics Society from 1959 to 1962. That is the root of the TESCREAL genealogy claim: Torres calls transhumanism ["eugenics on steroids"](/e/emile-torres) because it aims not only to improve the species but to replace it with something superior. The same hope drives the [singularity](/e/singularitarianism) and the dream of cosmic expansion in [cosmism](/e/cosmism).

**Strongest objection / best reply.** Transhumanists insist they have broken with the coercion of the past. Theirs is [liberal eugenics](/e/liberal-eugenics): individual choice, not state programs. That break is real and matters. Critics answer that in a world of radically unequal access, "choice" can do the sorting that governments once did, and the question of *who decides what counts as improvement* has simply moved from the state to the market.
