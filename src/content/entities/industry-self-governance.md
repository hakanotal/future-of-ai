Labs writing their own rules. It is the dominant way frontier AI has been governed so far, and the most tested.

**The idea.** The inventions are genuinely novel. OpenAI began as a nonprofit whose board could fire the CEO. Anthropic created a [Long-Term Benefit Trust](/e/long-term-benefit-trust) of financially disinterested trustees who elect a growing share of its board, and a [Responsible Scaling Policy](/e/responsible-scaling-policy) that commits it to pause or add safeguards as models cross danger thresholds. At the 2024 Seoul summit, frontier labs signed voluntary safety commitments.

**The question it leaves you with.** Would you accept this model for any other powerful industry? Imagine pharmaceutical companies deciding for themselves which drugs were safe to release, with a trust of five people as the backstop.

**Where it goes in the story.** The model's defining test came in November 2023, when OpenAI's nonprofit board fired Sam Altman. Within five days, 745 of 770 employees and Microsoft had reversed it ([board crisis](/e/openai-board-crisis)). In 2025 OpenAI [restructured](/e/openai-recapitalization) into a public benefit corporation still nominally controlled by its foundation. And in 2026 Anthropic's self-imposed limits on military use ran into the Pentagon ([dispute](/e/anthropic-pentagon-dispute)), which forced the question of whether a company's safety commitments can survive a state that wants them gone.

**Strongest objection / best reply.** Defenders say self-governance is what exists while law catches up, and that labs have sometimes held lines that governments didn't. Critics reply that rules the rule-maker can rewrite are policies, not governance, and that the people most affected have no seat in any of these structures.
