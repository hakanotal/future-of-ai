The twentieth-century project of "improving" the human population by controlling who reproduces. Its *negative* form sterilized or killed those deemed unfit; its *positive* form encouraged the "fit" to have more children.

**The idea.** Its programs were not fringe. California forcibly sterilized some 20,000 people between 1909 and 1979; Nazi Germany sterilized and murdered hundreds of thousands. Underneath was an assumption that evolution means *improvement*, not adaptation, and a question nobody could answer fairly: *who decides what counts as improved?* Historically the answer was racial purity, intelligence and physical fitness, as judged by the powerful.

**The question it leaves you with.** Is there a version of "making humanity better" that doesn't need someone to decide which people, traits or futures count for more? If you think [liberal eugenics](/e/liberal-eugenics), free individual choice, is that version, what happens when only the rich can choose?

**Where it goes in the story.** [Julian Huxley](/e/julian-huxley), who gave [transhumanism](/e/transhumanism) its name in 1957, was president of the British Eugenics Society from 1959 to 1962 and praised "artificial selection" as faster and "much less wasteful" than natural selection. That lineage is the core of the [TESCREAL](/e/tescreal) genealogy argument. Critics hear echoes elsewhere: in [Beckstead](/e/nick-beckstead)'s hedged suggestion that rich-country lives may matter more for the far future, and in the 1996 email for which [Bostrom apologized](/e/bostrom-email-apology) in 2023.

**Strongest objection / best reply.** Transhumanists and longtermists reject coercion outright, and guilt by ancestry is not an argument. Gebru and Torres reply that they are not claiming identical beliefs, only an inherited habit of reasoning that ranks some people's capacities, or some people's visions of the future, above others'. Whether that habit survives is a question to ask of each argument, not of each person.
