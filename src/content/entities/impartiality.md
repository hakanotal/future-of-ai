The principle that a person's suffering counts the same wherever and whoever they are: your child or a stranger's, down the street or across the world, and, if you go all the way, now or a thousand years from now.

**The idea.** [Singer](/e/peter-singer)'s [drowning child](/e/drowning-child) makes it vivid: distance doesn't dissolve responsibility. [Effective altruism](/e/effective-altruism) builds on it. [Longtermism](/e/longtermism) extends it from space to time, and that single extension changes everything, because there are so many more people in the future than in the present.

**The question it leaves you with.** Do you owe your own child more than a stranger's? Almost everyone says yes. If impartiality is right, that preference is a bias. If the preference is right, where does partiality stop: family, nation, species, generation?

**Where it goes in the story.** Impartiality is the source of both EA's moral force and its strangest conclusions: that a public defender is failing the people her salary could have saved ([replaceability](/e/replaceability)), or that today's poor are outweighed by tomorrow's trillions. The public-defender anecdote shows the tension: the student's anger was treated as partiality to be corrected.

**Strongest objection / best reply.** Relationship-based ethics (feminist care ethics, many religious and Indigenous traditions) holds that obligations come from particular bonds, not only from abstract welfare. Impartialists reply that such bonds have also justified ignoring outsiders for most of history. The honest position may be that we need both, and a way to decide between them that no formula provides.
