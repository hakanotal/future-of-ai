The most famous thought experiment in modern ethics, from [Peter Singer](/e/peter-singer)'s [Famine, Affluence, and Morality](/e/famine-affluence-and-morality), written in Oxford in 1971 as refugees fled famine and war in East Bengal.

**The idea.** You walk past a shallow pond and see a small child drowning. Wading in will cost you something trivial; in Singer's original, it will get your clothes muddy (the "expensive shoes" of later retellings are an upgrade). Of course you wade in. Then the turn: a child dying of hunger thousands of kilometres away can be saved at a similarly small cost. Why is that money still in your pocket? The principle underneath: *if it is in our power to prevent something very bad from happening, without thereby sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, we ought to do it.*

**The question it leaves you with.** Does distance dissolve moral responsibility? If not, how much of what you own is really yours? Try it concretely: what is the most expensive thing you bought this year, and what would it have bought at a [GiveWell](/e/givewell)-recommended charity?

**Where it goes in the story.** It took about forty years for the argument to become a movement. [Toby Ord](/e/toby-ord) read Singer and did the arithmetic; [effective altruism](/e/effective-altruism) turned the pond into a pipeline. And the movement's later quarrels are already inside the scene. Which child? The one in front of you, the one abroad, or, for [longtermism](/e/longtermism), one who will not be born for a thousand years? And who decides?

**Strongest objection / best reply.** Critics say the analogy flattens politics: a drowning child has no government, no history of colonization, no local movement already at work. Treating global poverty as a pond invites rescue from outside rather than solidarity. Singer's reply is that none of that makes it acceptable to walk past.
