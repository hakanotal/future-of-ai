In 2026 the question "who decides what safe AI means?" left the seminar room and went to court.

**What happened.** [Anthropic](/e/anthropic) had written limits into its usage terms: its models should not be used for fully autonomous weapons or domestic mass surveillance. The [Department of Defense](/e/us-department-of-defense) wanted access for "all lawful purposes". On 27 February 2026 the President directed federal agencies to stop using Anthropic's technology; on 5 March the Pentagon designated the company a supply-chain risk to national security, a label normally reserved for foreign adversaries and never before applied to an American company. On 27 August a federal judge in California ruled the designation "unlawful retaliation" in violation of the First Amendment, and "arbitrary and capricious". The litigation continues.

**The question it leaves you with.** Should a private company be able to refuse its own government a use of its technology on ethical grounds? Now reverse it: should a government be able to force one to comply? Most people's answers depend on which company and which government, and that is exactly the problem a rule is supposed to solve.

**Where it goes in the story.** It is the sharpest collision yet between "safety" as a lab's self-imposed value ([industry self-governance](/e/industry-self-governance)) and "security" as a state's prerogative ([AI security](/e/ai-security)). It also shows the limits of both. The company's lines were its own policy, not law; the state's claim was about security, but whose?

**Strongest objection / best reply.** One side argues that in a democracy, elected governments, not corporations, should decide how the military uses technology. The other replies that the law already limits governments, including on surveillance, and that a company declining to build something is not a threat to national security.
