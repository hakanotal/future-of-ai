The decision rule underneath almost everything in this story: for each option, multiply how good each outcome would be by how likely it is, add them up, and choose the option with the highest total.

**The idea.** It is how insurers and poker players think, and it is genuinely powerful. A charity that saves one life for sure is worse than one with a 50% chance of saving four. [Effective altruism](/e/effective-altruism) used it to rank charities; [longtermism](/e/longtermism) used it to rank the future. The trouble starts when the numbers get extreme. [Torture vs. Dust Specks](/e/torture-vs-dust-specks) multiplies a tiny harm by an unimaginably large number of people until it outweighs fifty years of torture.

**The question it leaves you with.** A stranger says: "Give me your wallet and I will use my magic powers to create 10^50 happy lives. Yes, the chance I am telling the truth is tiny, but surely not *that* tiny." Expected utility says pay. This is [Pascal's mugging](/e/fanaticism), and every serious longtermist has had to answer it. What is your answer, and does it also rule out the [10^16 lives](/e/strong-longtermism-2021) at stake in a small cut to extinction risk?

**Where it goes in the story.** The collection [The Good It Promises, the Harm It Does](/e/good-it-promises-harm-it-does) argues that EA passes off one contested philosophical filter as the property of reason and evidence themselves, then hands the calculator to elite experts whose judgments are treated as value-free. [Guillaume Verdon](/e/guillaume-verdon)'s e/acc, from the other side, keeps the maximizing and swaps the quantity.

**Strongest objection / best reply.** Defenders point out that refusing to calculate is also a choice, usually a worse one: without numbers, charity goes to whatever tugs hardest on the heart. Critics accept that and ask a narrower question. Not "should we calculate?" but "when a calculation reaches a conclusion that almost every human rejects, which one is broken?"
