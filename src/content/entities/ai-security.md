The reframing of AI risk as a problem of national security: cyberattacks, bioweapons, military advantage, and above all the race with China.

**The idea.** In February 2025, three days after the [Paris summit](/e/paris-ai-action-summit), the UK renamed its AI Safety Institute the [AI Security Institute](/e/uk-ai-security-institute). In June the US Commerce Secretary turned the US AI Safety Institute into the [Center for AI Standards and Innovation](/e/us-ai-safety-institute), focused on "demonstrable risks, such as cybersecurity, biosecurity, and chemical weapons". The security frame has deep EA roots: [Open Philanthropy](/e/open-philanthropy) founded [CSET](/e/cset) in 2019 with $55 million to build AI-security expertise in Washington, and Leopold Aschenbrenner's [Situational Awareness](/e/situational-awareness) cast AGI as a US–China contest.

**The question it leaves you with.** Security for whom? A state's security can mean using AI for surveillance and autonomous weapons; a citizen's security can mean being protected from exactly that. When the two conflict, who decides?

**Where it goes in the story.** The conflict became literal in 2026. [Anthropic](/e/anthropic) refused to let the Pentagon use its models for fully autonomous weapons or domestic mass surveillance; the Department of Defense labeled it a supply-chain risk, a designation normally reserved for foreign adversaries; a federal judge called that unlawful retaliation. The [dispute](/e/anthropic-pentagon-dispute) marks the point where "safety" as a lab's value ran into "security" as a state's prerogative.

**Strongest objection / best reply.** Supporters argue the security frame finally made governments take AI risk seriously, and that concrete threats are easier to govern than speculative ones. Critics reply that security framing tends to move decisions behind classification and into defense budgets, the least democratic corners of government, and makes the [race](/e/ai-race) the organizing principle.
