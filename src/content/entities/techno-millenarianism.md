The scholarly argument that technological futurism is not only *like* religion but reuses its structure. It is the "hidden faith" in this site's title.

**The idea.** The historian [David Noble](/e/david-noble) argued in [The Religion of Technology](/e/religion-of-technology) (1997) that Western engineering has been driven since the Middle Ages by a dream of redemption: recovering the perfection lost in the Fall. [Robert Geraci](/e/robert-geraci)'s [Apocalyptic AI](/e/apocalyptic-ai) (2010) showed that AI futurists like Kurzweil and Moravec reproduce apocalyptic theology almost point by point: a fallen, mortal present; a coming rupture (the Singularity); and a transcendent digital afterlife. The anthropologist [Beth Singler](/e/beth-singler) studies how AI stories take on religious shapes: blessing, apocalypse, deification.

**The question it leaves you with.** Map it yourself. What is this story's heaven ([posthuman](/e/posthuman) flourishing, [cosmic](/e/cosmism) expansion)? Its hell (extinction)? Its salvation ([alignment](/e/ai-alignment), upload)? Its priesthood? If you can fill in every blank, is that a coincidence?

**Where it goes in the story.** Some cases are explicit. [Ben Goertzel](/e/ben-goertzel)'s [Cosmist Manifesto](/e/cosmist-manifesto) promises to achieve "by scientific means most of the promises of religions", including resurrection. [Anthony Levandowski](/e/anthony-levandowski) registered a church, [Way of the Future](/e/way-of-the-future), for "the realization of a Godhead based on Artificial Intelligence". Most cases are implicit, which is what makes the frame useful: a faith that doesn't know it is one is hard to argue with.

**Strongest objection / best reply.** Believers reply that calling something religious is a way of dismissing it without engaging its arguments; a forecast can have a religious shape and still be right. True. The frame's value is not to refute the forecasts but to explain their pull, and to ask why a few prophets should get to decide for everyone.
