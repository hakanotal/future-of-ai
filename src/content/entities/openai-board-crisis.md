Five days in November 2023 that tested whether anyone could actually stop a frontier AI lab.

**What happened.** On Friday 17 November, OpenAI's nonprofit board, whose legal duty was to the mission rather than to investors, removed CEO [Sam Altman](/e/sam-altman), saying he had not been "consistently candid". The four who voted him out were [Ilya Sutskever](/e/ilya-sutskever), [Adam D'Angelo](/e/adam-dangelo), [Helen Toner](/e/helen-toner) and [Tasha McCauley](/e/tasha-mccauley); Toner and McCauley were seen as close to EA and AI-safety circles. Reporting described a split over speed versus safety. [Microsoft](/e/microsoft), with about $13 billion committed, offered to hire Altman and anyone who followed. 745 of 770 employees signed a letter threatening to leave; Sutskever signed it and apologized. [Emmett Shear](/e/emmett-shear) was interim CEO for about three days. On 22 November Altman returned, and a new board under [Bret Taylor](/e/bret-taylor) replaced the members who had removed him, except D'Angelo.

**The question it leaves you with.** The board did what it was designed to do. It lost to the employees and the investors. So who was actually in charge, and would the answer have been different if the board had been right?

**Where it goes in the story.** This is the defining test of [industry self-governance](/e/industry-self-governance). It led to OpenAI's 2025 [recapitalization](/e/openai-recapitalization) and hardened the [race](/e/ai-race) logic: safety that slows a lab down can be overruled by the people whose jobs and equity depend on it.

**Strongest objection / best reply.** Many argue the board handled it badly: vague reasons, no plan, poor communication. That is fair. But the structural lesson survives the tactical one: a governance mechanism that can only be used once, and fails when it is, is not much of a mechanism.
