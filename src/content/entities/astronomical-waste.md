[Nick Bostrom](/e/nick-bostrom)'s 2003 argument, in the paper of the [same name](/e/astronomical-waste-2003), that the stakes of the future are so large that delay itself is a moral catastrophe.

**The idea.** The reachable universe could support vast numbers of lives. Bostrom estimated that every century of delay in settling our local supercluster forfeits the potential for roughly 10^38 human lives: about 10^29 per second. His conclusion is subtler than "go fast": because extinction would lose *everything*, reducing existential risk even slightly matters far more than speeding up progress. The better-known figure of 10^58 possible future minds, many of them digital, comes from his later work (2013–2014), not this paper.

**The question it leaves you with.** Can a life that will never exist be "lost"? If your answer is yes, how does the loss of 10^29 possible people per second compare with the death of one actual person today?

**Where it goes in the story.** This is the arithmetic that turned [existential risk](/e/existential-risk) into [longtermism](/e/longtermism)'s top priority, and it draws its numbers from [cosmism](/e/cosmism)'s dream of expansion. Greaves and MacAskill's [strong longtermism](/e/strong-longtermism-2021) uses far more conservative figures and still reaches similar conclusions.

**Strongest objection / best reply.** Critics point to [fanaticism](/e/fanaticism): numbers this large can justify anything. Bostrom's defenders reply that the argument points toward *caution*, since it favors safety over speed. The irony of the story is that the same numbers were later used to justify a race.
