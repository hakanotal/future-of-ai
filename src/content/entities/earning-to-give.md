The early EA career strategy: instead of working for a charity, take the highest-paying job you can, often in finance, and give most of the money to the most effective causes.

**The idea.** [William MacAskill](/e/william-macaskill) made the case to students in the early 2010s and in [Doing Good Better](/e/doing-good-better): your career is your biggest moral decision. The argument rests on [replaceability](/e/replaceability): your seat at a charity will be filled by someone roughly as good, while your seat at a trading firm goes to someone who won't donate. Its most controversial extension, [moral offsetting](/e/moral-offsetting), suggests even a harmful job can be justified if the donations outweigh the damage.

**The question it leaves you with.** A widely retold (and unsourced) story: an EA-minded student tells a law student that becoming a public defender is, in effect, letting people die, because a corporate salary could fund bed nets. She is angry; he takes her anger as proof she can't handle impartial logic. Is her anger a bias to overcome, or information the calculation is missing?

**Where it goes in the story.** Its poster child was [Sam Bankman-Fried](/e/sam-bankman-fried), redirected by MacAskill from animal welfare to Wall Street and then to crypto. The [FTX collapse](/e/ftx-collapse) became the strategy's defining test. [80,000 Hours](/e/eighty-thousand-hours) had de-emphasized earning to give years earlier, recommending direct work, increasingly on AI.

**Strongest objection / best reply.** The pattern critics see: a chain of individually plausible steps, a conclusion ordinary intuition rejects, and the rejection treated as irrationality. Defenders reply that thousands of people quietly earn and give, and that the strategy was always meant to operate within ordinary moral limits. The dispute is whether the framework itself supplies those limits.
