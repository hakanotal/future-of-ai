Nick Beckstead's 2013 Rutgers PhD dissertation, *On the Overwhelming Importance of Shaping the Far Future*, supervised by [Larry Temkin](/e/larry-temkin): an early systematic case for [longtermism](/e/longtermism).

Its most quoted passage is often quoted out of context, so here it is in full: "saving lives in poor countries may have significantly smaller ripple effects than saving and improving lives in rich countries. Why? Richer countries have substantially more innovation, and their workers are much more economically productive… it now seems more plausible to me that saving a life in a rich country is substantially more important than saving a life in a poor country, other things being equal."

The hedges matter ("may", "more plausible to me", "other things being equal"), and Beckstead presents it as one "surprising moral conclusion" among several, next to the claim that the far-future view had made him rank factory-farm suffering as much less important than before. Critics reply that the hedges do not change the direction of the reasoning, which ranks human lives by their usefulness to the future.

Beckstead later worked at [Open Philanthropy](/e/open-philanthropy) and ran the [FTX Future Fund](/e/ftx-future-fund).

**A question to take away:** when an argument's conclusion echoes a hierarchy with an ugly history, is that a reason to doubt the argument, or only a reason to check it twice?
