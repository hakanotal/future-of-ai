A risk that would destroy humanity's long-term potential for good: extinction, or an unrecoverable collapse or dystopia. [Nick Bostrom](/e/nick-bostrom) named the category in 2002.

**The idea.** Most catastrophes, however terrible, leave survivors who can rebuild. An existential catastrophe forecloses the whole future. [Toby Ord](/e/toby-ord)'s [The Precipice](/e/the-precipice) (2020) put the total chance this century at about 1 in 6, and the breakdown is the point: asteroids and supervolcanoes are tiny, while our own inventions dominate. Unaligned AI is about 1 in 10; engineered pandemics about 1 in 30; nuclear war and climate change about 1 in 1,000 each.

**The question it leaves you with.** Is extinction worse than the deaths of everyone alive, and if so, by how much? If you think "far worse, because of all the people who would never exist", you have accepted longtermism's central premise. If you think "about the same", you have not.

**Where it goes in the story.** Existential risk is [longtermism](/e/longtermism)'s top practical priority and the origin of EA's specific meaning of [AI safety](/e/ai-safety). It is also the root of what [Timnit Gebru](/e/timnit-gebru) calls a paradox: the fear that AI could end humanity became a reason to [race](/e/ai-race) to build it first, before someone less careful does. By 2025, governments had quietly traded the vocabulary of existential risk for [AI security](/e/ai-security).

**Strongest objection / best reply.** Critics say the probabilities are guesses dressed as measurements, and that a focus on hypothetical extinction drains attention from certain, present harms. Ord's reply is that he gives the numbers so they can be argued with, and that a 1-in-6 chance of losing everything would be treated as an emergency in any other domain.
