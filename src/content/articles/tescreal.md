## Part I: The Drowning Child

Princeton, New Jersey, 1971. Autumn leaves are still turning on the tree-lined paths of the campus. At his desk sits a forty-five-year-old Australian philosophy professor in thin-framed glasses, quiet, precise, not yet famous. His name is [Peter Singer](/e/peter-singer), and he is writing what looks like an ordinary journal article.

It was not ordinary. Published in the spring of 1972 in *Philosophy & Public Affairs* under the title **"[Famine, Affluence, and Morality](/e/famine-affluence-and-morality)"** it posed one of the most uncomfortable moral questions of the twentieth century.

[Singer](/e/peter-singer) sets a simple scene. You are walking to work. Beside the path there is a shallow pond, and in that pond a small child is drowning. Nobody else is around. Only you. If you wade in, the expensive shoes on your feet will be ruined.

The question: would you let that child drown to save your shoes?

Of course not. No sane person watches a child die for a pair of shoes.

Then comes [Singer](/e/peter-singer)'s second question, the one that dynamites centuries of moral philosophy. Suppose that child is thousands of kilometres away, in 1971, fleeing the floods and famine in East Bengal, and suppose a hundred dollars from you would keep them alive. Why is the hundred dollars still in your pocket? **Does distance dissolve moral responsibility?**

His argument is disarmingly tight: *if it is in your power to prevent something very bad from happening, without thereby sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, you ought to do it.* Follow that through and you arrive somewhere radical, nearly everyone living comfortably ought to be giving away a large share of their income, continuously, for life.

The reaction split along a line that has never closed. Some called [Singer](/e/peter-singer) the most important moral philosopher of the modern age. Others called him a dangerous provocateur. Both camps agreed on one thing: the logic was hard to escape.

It took roughly forty years for the argument to become a movement.

## Part II: Oxford, and the Question Singer Never Asked

Oxford, England, 2006. In one of the most prestigious universities in the world, a young philosophy student is devouring [Singer](/e/peter-singer)'s work. His name is [**Toby Ord**](/e/toby-ord), Australian family, quiet, modest, and intellectually enormously ambitious.

Walking Oxford's ancient stone corridors, [Ord](/e/toby-ord) did a calculation. On a British academic's salary, over the course of a working life, he could give away enough money to save, by the best available estimates, something on the order of ten thousand lives.

But to *which* charity? This was the question [Singer](/e/peter-singer) had not asked and that [Ord](/e/toby-ord) could not stop asking. **If you want to do good, how do you do the most good?** Because charities are not equal. Some save dozens of lives per dollar bracket; others, with the same money, produce almost no measurable effect at all.

[Ord](/e/toby-ord) pledged to give away everything he earned above a modest personal threshold, and in **2009**, together with fellow philosopher [**William MacAskill**](/e/william-macaskill) and physician [Bernadette Young](/e/bernadette-young), founded [**Giving What We Can**](/e/give-what-we-can) at Oxford. It began as a giving society of twenty-three members with one core commitment: pledge at least 10% of your income, and direct it on the basis of evidence to the most effective organisations available.

[MacAskill](/e/william-macaskill), Scottish, from Glasgow, then in his early twenties and still finishing his doctorate, wanted the frame widened. Not only *how much should we give?* but *how should we live in order to do the most good?* Career choice, use of time, even diet belonged in the calculation. In **2011** he and [Benjamin Todd](/e/benjamin-todd) founded [**80,000 Hours**](/e/eighty-thousand-hours) (the number of working hours in a typical career), and that same year the movement that had been gathering around these ideas acquired its name: [**effective altruism**](/e/effective-altruism). In **2012** the [**Centre for Effective Altruism**](/e/centre-for-effective-altruism) was incorporated at Oxford as the umbrella body.


Hidden in the seed of the movement, however, was code that would later grow into an enormous forest, or, depending on your view, an enormous fire. Because inside the phrase **"do the most good"** sit three unanswered questions:

- **Good for whom?**
- **Over what time horizon?**
- **And decided by whom?**

Within ten years those questions would gather Silicon Valley's brightest minds, richest wallets, and most dangerous ideas around a single movement.

## Part III: The Measurable Good

San Francisco, 2007. Two young analysts at a hedge fund, [**Elie Hassenfeld**](/e/elie-hassenfeld) and [**Holden Karnofsky**](/e/holden-karnofsky), noticed that the charity world was in a dark age of transparency. In finance, every investment was dissected. In philanthropy, almost nothing was. They founded [**GiveWell**](/e/givewell) to evaluate charities with the same scrutiny: not by overhead ratios or glossy annual reports, but by evidence of outcomes.

[GiveWell](/e/givewell) became the reference point of the movement, and through the 2010s and 2020s it channelled hundreds of millions of dollars into evidence-backed programmes. Its recommendations are still dominated by unglamorous interventions, insecticide-treated bed nets against malaria (Against Malaria Foundation, Malaria Consortium), vitamin A supplementation (Helen Keller International), cash incentives for childhood vaccination (New Incentives). [GiveWell](/e/givewell)'s own rough estimate is that roughly **$3,500–$5,500** in donations to its top charities averts one death, a figure that shifts with country, season, and modelling assumptions, and that [GiveWell](/e/givewell) itself presents as an approximation rather than a price tag.

In 2014 Karnofsky co-founded [**Open Philanthropy**](/e/open-philanthropy) (renamed [**Coefficient Giving**](/e/open-philanthropy) in 2025) with [Good Ventures](/e/good-ventures), the foundation of Facebook co-founder [**Dustin Moskovitz**](/e/dustin-moskovitz) and [**Cari Tuna**](/e/cari-tuna), the movement's largest living donors. [Open Philanthropy](/e/open-philanthropy) has since directed more than **$4 billion** in grants.

This is the part of the story that is genuinely hard to argue with. [Singer](/e/peter-singer)'s [drowning child](/e/drowning-child) had stopped being a thought experiment and become a working mechanism. Ordinary people, skipping a coffee, were keeping alive children they would never meet. Pure, measurable, essentially uncontroversial good.

### The objection from the other side of the transaction

And yet the critique that cuts deepest does not come from philosophers. It comes from the people on the receiving end.

A Ugandan farmer, [**Anthony Kalulu**](/e/anthony-kalulu), has written about his disillusionment with EA's promise to the ultra-poor. Traditional philanthropy, he argues, keeps people in poverty as passive recipients, only about 1% of global anti-poverty funding reaches grassroots organisations in the global South directly. EA's pitch sounded to him like a corrective. He found it was worse: *"In the eyes of the poor, traditional philanthropy and EA alike have only pushed their own predetermined solutions which aren't rooted in the lived experiences of the extreme poor."* Because the solutions are imposed, he argues, they are short-lived. And of the organisations [GiveWell](/e/givewell) names as most effective, not one is African, though much of the work happens in Africa.

[Torres](/e/emile-torres), a former effective altruist, puts the structural version of the point bluntly: philanthropy transfers **wealth** from the global North to the global South while keeping the **power**, power to decide what counts, where money goes, how funds are used. Critics call the surrounding apparatus the *nonprofit industrial complex*: tax-deductible giving that papers over the structural inequities that produced the wealth in the first place, while the same actors lobby to gut public services.

The essay collection *[**The Good It Promises, the Harm It Does**](/e/good-it-promises-harm-it-does)* sharpens this into a claim about epistemology: "effectiveness" gets pushed through a very specific philosophical filter, expected utility, which then presents itself as the exclusive property of reason and evidence. Decisions about what is and isn't effective are handed to elite experts whose judgments are treated as value-free. In her foreword, [**Amia Srinivasan**](/e/amia-srinivasan) notes what is missing: EA has found almost nothing worth learning in the historically deep movements for the rights of working-class people, people of colour, Indigenous people, women, incarcerated people, disabled people, and people living under colonial and authoritarian rule. For most effective altruists, she writes, these are at best examples of *ineffective* attempts to do good, "not political formations from which to learn, with which to create coalition or to join." Her verdict: EA "promises us a better world while implicitly encouraging us to accept the world more or less as it is."

**The defenders' answer**, which deserves stating: bed nets work. Vitamin A works. Cash works. A movement that moved billions toward interventions with the strongest evidence base in development economics has a record that is not erased by its blind spots, and "fund grassroots organising instead" is a proposal whose own effects are much harder to measure, which is precisely the disagreement, not a rebuttal of it.

## Part IV: Earning to Give

Cambridge, Massachusetts, 2011 or 2012, accounts differ. On the MIT campus there is a curly-haired physics undergraduate who lives in shorts and t-shirts, cannot keep a room tidy, and can hold three simultaneous chess games in his head. His name is [**Sam Bankman-Fried**](/e/sam-bankman-fried). His parents are both Stanford Law professors who raised him on consequentialism, the view that the moral worth of an act is measured by its outcomes. He was, by temperament, a calculator; everything went through cost-benefit analysis.

That year a guest speaker came to campus: [**William MacAskill**](/e/william-macaskill). His message to the students was that **your career is the biggest moral decision of your life**, and that if you are strong at mathematics and analytical thinking, you may do far more good by *not* working at a charity. Go into finance. Work on Wall Street. Earn as much as possible. Give most of it away.

The strategy had a name: **earning to give.**

Bankman-Fried later said the argument redirected him. Until then he had been thinking about working directly in animal welfare. [MacAskill](/e/william-macaskill)'s logic was irresistible to someone who already thought in expected values: *handing out pamphlets makes me one person's worth of difference; earning enough to fund the salaries of many staff makes me many people's worth of difference.* Mathematically, [earning to give](/e/earning-to-give) wins.

He joined [**Jane Street Capital**](/e/jane-street) in 2013–14, began donating a large share of his income, and became the movement's model citizen. [80,000 Hours](/e/eighty-thousand-hours) marketed him as the exemplar of [earning to give](/e/earning-to-give). Nobody yet knew that within a few years he would sit at the centre of one of the largest financial frauds in history.

### The logic downstream

Earning to give did not stop at trading firms. [MacAskill](/e/william-macaskill) argued that even morally controversial careers could be justified, work for a petrochemical company or an arms manufacturer, the reasoning goes, and you can offset the damage you do and then some through donations. Critics called it moral carbon offsetting.

The doctrine also produced a certain kind of conversation, of which many versions circulate. In one widely retold story, an EA-sympathetic graduate student told a law student that she should not become a public defender: she should go to a corporate firm and donate to anti-malaria charities instead. His argument was replaceability, if she didn't take the Wall Street job somebody else would, and that person wouldn't donate, whereas her public-defender seat would be filled by someone roughly as good. Therefore, by choosing to defend the indigent, she was effectively killing the people her donated salary could have saved. When she became angry, he took the anger as proof that she could not handle impartial logic.

This is where a sceptical reader should pause, because the structure of that argument reappears at every scale of this story: **a chain of individually plausible steps arriving at a conclusion that ordinary moral intuition rejects, with the rejection itself treated as evidence of irrationality.**

## Part V: Alameda, FTX, and the Collapse

Berkeley, California, 2017. In a small apartment behind Silicon Valley, Bankman-Fried sits in front of six monitors. He has left Jane Street; after a short stint at the [Centre for Effective Altruism](/e/centre-for-effective-altruism) he founds his own firm in **November 2017**: [**Alameda Research**](/e/alameda-research), a quantitative crypto trading operation, algorithms detecting price differences across markets and profiting in milliseconds.

The early days were chaotic. People slept on the office floor. Accounting was not properly kept. But money was pouring in: the 2017–18 crypto bull run produced enormous spreads between Asian and Western exchanges, and Alameda harvested them.

The red flags were already flying. According to *TIME*'s 2022 investigation, early Alameda employees sent serious warnings about Bankman-Fried's management to leaders of the [effective altruism](/e/effective-altruism) community throughout 2018 and 2019, dishonesty, sloppy accounting, outright rejection of corporate controls, inappropriate relationships with subordinates, and use of employee funds in risky positions. Those warnings reached [**William MacAskill**](/e/william-macaskill), [**Holden Karnofsky**](/e/holden-karnofsky), [Nick Beckstead](/e/nick-beckstead), and other senior EA figures. What happened? They were minimised as ordinary startup friction, or rationalised, *his style is unusual, but look at the results.* **Nobody hit the brakes.**

In **2019** came the second and much larger move: [**FTX**](/e/ftx), a crypto exchange, headquartered initially in Hong Kong and later in the Bahamas. Low fees, aggressive product design, and vast marketing, Super Bowl ads, a partnership with Tom Brady, naming rights to the Miami Heat arena. [FTX](/e/ftx) was everywhere. At its peak in 2021–22 Bankman-Fried's personal fortune was estimated at roughly **$26.5 billion**, making him one of the richest people in America.

And he was keeping his word, at least in public. **$5.2 million** to pro-Biden super PACs in 2020; nearly **$40 million** to Democratic causes in the 2022 cycle; millions into [AI safety](/e/ai-safety) and biosecurity; a signature on the Giving Pledge.

Then, in early **2022**, the crown: the [**FTX Future Fund**](/e/ftx-future-fund), which by September 2022 had committed roughly **$160 million** in grants and investments to more than a hundred organisations, of which around **$36.5 million** went directly to [effective altruism](/e/effective-altruism) organisations. On its advisory team sat a very familiar name: [**William MacAskill**](/e/william-macaskill). The man who had sold Bankman-Fried on [earning to give](/e/earning-to-give) a decade earlier was now sitting at the table deciding where the money went.

Behind the curtain, a different game. Billions of dollars of [FTX](/e/ftx) customer deposits were being funnelled quietly to [Alameda Research](/e/alameda-research) and used for personal investments, political donations, and luxury real estate. Alameda's CEO was [**Caroline Ellison**](/e/caroline-ellison), Harvard-trained mathematician, Bankman-Fried's business partner and for a period his romantic partner, who would later testify that he directed the crimes, and cooperate with prosecutors against him.

To anyone outside in the first half of 2022, [FTX](/e/ftx) was the most trustworthy, most transparent, most ethical exchange in crypto.

### Eleven days

- **2 November 2022.** *CoinDesk* publishes a leaked Alameda balance sheet. The bulk of Alameda's assets are not real money but **FTT**, a token [FTX](/e/ftx) itself had created, including some $3.66 billion of "unlocked FTT." Alameda was, in substance, a paper castle financing itself with its own paper.
- **6 November.** [Binance](/e/binance) founder [**Changpeng Zhao**](/e/changpeng-zhao) announces he will sell roughly **$529 million** of FTT. A classic bank run begins; [FTX](/e/ftx) customers rush the exits.
- **8 November.** Bankman-Fried announces a rescue deal with [Binance](/e/binance). Zhao looks at the books and walks away.
- **11 November 2022.** [FTX](/e/ftx) files for bankruptcy. Roughly **$8 billion** in customer funds has evaporated. More than a million people lose some or all of their savings. A $26.5 billion fortune goes to zero overnight, and the brightest star of [effective altruism](/e/effective-altruism) is recast as one of the great financial frauds in history. Anthony Scaramucci called him "the Bernie Madoff of crypto."

Bankman-Fried was convicted on all seven counts on **2 November 2023** and sentenced to **25 years** on **28 March 2024**.

### The confession

The greater shock was not the collapse but what came after it. Days later, in private messages to a *Vox* reporter, Bankman-Fried summarised the ethics of the movement that had made him:

> **"Ethics stuff, [it's] mostly a front... a dumb game we woke Westerners play where we say all the right shibboleths and so everyone likes us."**

*Shibboleth*: an ancient Hebrew term for a password that proves you belong to the group.

He was admitting, explicitly, that he had used the language of [effective altruism](/e/effective-altruism) not from moral conviction but to manufacture social acceptance and credibility. The rhetoric of doing good had become, in his hands, an instrument of reputation engineering.

[Torres](/e/emile-torres) offers a darker reading than "he was a hypocrite." Bankman-Fried, he argues, was a utilitarian and a longtermist, and on that view *nothing* is intrinsically wrong, not fraud, not lying, not theft. It depends entirely on outcomes. Sitting in the Bahamas funding longtermist projects, the reasoning available to him was: *sure, I'm committing a little fraud, but what I'm funding will have such a vast positive impact on humanity's long-term future.* On a strict utilitarian view, if enough welfare is produced by defrauding people, defrauding people is not merely permitted, it is obligatory.

[MacAskill](/e/william-macaskill) later went on Sam Harris's podcast to argue that Bankman-Fried's actions were contrary to EA's values and better explained by hubris. Whether that is convincing depends on whether you think the hubris is incidental to the framework or generated by it.

## Part VI: Longtermism: From Today's Poor to Tomorrow's Trillions

We now have to follow the other branch of the story. Because while [Singer](/e/peter-singer)'s [drowning child](/e/drowning-child) and [Ord](/e/toby-ord)'s evidence-based giving were doing concrete, measurable good, something was quietly and radically evolving in the early 2010s. The question *whom do we save today?* was giving way to a much larger, much more abstract, and much more dangerous one: **how do we save the trillions of people who will be born in the future?**

### The philosophical ancestor

In **1984**, the Oxford philosopher [**Derek Parfit**](/e/derek-parfit) published *Reasons and Persons*, containing some of the most disorienting thought experiments in the discipline. His question: do we have moral obligations to people who will exist in the future?

[Parfit](/e/derek-parfit) said yes, and argued it with nuclear waste. Dump waste in the desert rather than storing it properly and, centuries from now, leaking radiation will harm people not yet born. That harm is not morally acceptable merely because the victims do not exist yet.

Up to this point, most people agree. Then [Parfit](/e/derek-parfit) took a further step and planted the seed of what would become "the hinge of history": *if future people matter morally, and if their number could be astronomical, then the long-run consequences of our actions matter far more than the short-run ones.*

### The machinery

[MacAskill](/e/william-macaskill) and [Ord](/e/toby-ord) took that argument and placed it at the centre of [effective altruism](/e/effective-altruism). Around **2017** they formulated the term [**longtermism**](/e/longtermism).

In **2020** [Ord](/e/toby-ord) published *[**The Precipice: Existential Risk and the Future of Humanity**](/e/the-precipice)*, calculating humanity's extinction probabilities one by one. His own estimate: roughly a **one-in-six** chance that humanity suffers an existential catastrophe within the next hundred years. Russian-roulette odds, and in his model the largest threats are not asteroids or natural disasters but our own creations: **unaligned artificial intelligence, engineered pandemics, nuclear war.**

Oxford, 2020. The pandemic has emptied the streets, but inside the [**Future of Humanity Institute**](/e/future-of-humanity-institute), founded by the Swedish philosopher [**Nick Bostrom**](/e/nick-bostrom) in 2005, a small group of philosophers and mathematicians is planning humanity's next ten thousand, hundred thousand, or billion years. [Bostrom](/e/nick-bostrom)'s *[**Superintelligence**](/e/superintelligence)* (2014) had already treated AI-driven human extinction with academic seriousness and hit Silicon Valley like an earthquake; after reading it, [**Elon Musk**](/e/elon-musk) began calling AI an existential threat to humanity.

In **2022**, at the exact moment [FTX](/e/ftx) was at its peak, [MacAskill](/e/william-macaskill) published *[**What We Owe the Future**](/e/what-we-owe-the-future)*. It was the intellectual turning point of the movement, shifting its focus from saving today's poor to protecting tomorrow's trillions, and it rested on three premises:

1. **People who will be born in the future have the same moral value as people alive today.**
2. **If humanity does not go extinct, the number of future people can be expressed in the trillions**, and with space colonisation, the number becomes astronomical.
3. **Our actions today can determine whether those people exist at all, and how they live.**

Combine the three and you reach the conclusion: **positively influencing the long-term future is the key moral priority of our time.**

### The arithmetic, and where it leads

The numbers are the whole argument. In a 2021 paper, [MacAskill](/e/william-macaskill) and [**Hilary Greaves**](/e/hilary-greaves) work with an estimate on the order of **10²⁴** future people. It follows that *"a reduction in near-term extinction risk by one millionth of one percentage point would be equivalent in value to 10¹⁶ lives saved"*, ten quadrillion lives, from a rounding error in a probability. [Bostrom](/e/nick-bostrom)'s version, allowing for digital minds, runs to **10⁵⁸** potential future beings.

Against numbers like these, a billion people saved from famine is a rounding error. And that is not a rhetorical exaggeration, it is the literal structure of the expected-value calculation. Priority shifts, mathematically and inexorably, to existential risk: runaway AI, laboratory pandemics, nuclear war.

Silicon Valley clustered around it. [**Sam Altman**](/e/sam-altman), [**Peter Thiel**](/e/peter-thiel), [**Jaan Tallinn**](/e/jaan-tallinn), [**Vitalik Buterin**](/e/vitalik-buterin), [**Dustin Moskovitz**](/e/dustin-moskovitz); [Elon Musk](/e/elon-musk) approached [longtermism](/e/longtermism) from an angle congenial to his own vision and called *What We Owe the Future* "a close match for my philosophy." [Longtermism](/e/longtermism) was no longer an academic debate. It was a multi-billion-dollar investment thesis.

### Four objections

**1. You cannot predict the future.** What could a person in 1500 have done to improve the world of 2026? Almost certainly nothing. Human history is full of unforeseeable breaks. [Longtermism](/e/longtermism) speaks with confidence about the far future without properly pricing that chaos. (A related structural critique, from [B.V.E. Hyde](/e/bv-hyde), is that [longtermism](/e/longtermism) assumes an implausibly butterfly-effect view of history, overrating individual agency and underrating structural forces.)

**2. Today's emergencies get crowded out.** A growing share of EA-influenced budgets flows to [AI safety](/e/ai-safety) and existential-risk institutes; the share going to global health programmes shrinks in relative terms. The philosopher [Elizabeth Hupfer](/e/elizabeth-hupfer) formalised this as the "far-future priority objection."

**3. The ends justify the means.** [Longtermism](/e/longtermism) is consequentialist by construction. If trillions of future lives are at stake, and a small present evil secures that vast future good, is the small evil licensed? Bankman-Fried is the empirical test case, and the answer he arrived at is not reassuring.

**4. The intellectual genealogy.** The most uncomfortable objection: that [longtermism](/e/longtermism) shares an intellectual DNA with techno-utopianism and, in some respects, with the eugenics movement, with the idea that the genetic and cognitive qualities of the human species can and should be "improved," and that some people's intelligence, or some people's visions of the future, count for more than others'.

That fourth critique was made most forcefully by two people.

### The tells

It is worth dwelling on the specific commitments that make critics reach for the word "ideology" rather than "philosophy."

- [**Nick Beckstead**](/e/nick-beckstead), a longtermist who later ran the [FTX Future Fund](/e/ftx-future-fund), argued in his doctoral work that saving lives in rich countries should be prioritised over saving lives in poor countries, because *"rich countries have substantially more innovation and their workers are much more economically productive."*
- On **nature**, [MacAskill](/e/william-macaskill) has argued that EA's aim concerns promoting well-being only, and not, say, protecting biodiversity or conserving natural beauty for their own sakes. Preserving an endangered species is a legitimate EA activity because populations can be counted; the interdependence of an old-growth forest cannot be, so it falls outside the frame. Critics note the cost of this: soil degradation, monoculture collapse, and the sixth mass extinction are precisely the sort of slow, unquantifiable, interconnected damage the framework is built to ignore.
- Following the well-being calculus further produces the conclusion that the loss of wild-animal habitat may have been a net *positive*, because wild animals' lives plausibly contain more suffering than joy and are therefore "worse than nothing on average." Plants and fungi register as neutral. Put the two together and the net value of wild nature can come out negative.
- On **climate change**, [MacAskill](/e/william-macaskill) argues that even in a worst case of 7–10 °C warming, it is hard to see how this leads *directly* to civilisational collapse, and his argument for decarbonisation is partly that we should leave enough accessible fossil fuel in the ground to power a *second* industrial revolution if civilisation collapses and has to restart. [Torres](/e/emile-torres)'s response is to ask the reader to reflect on what the first industrial revolution did to the planet: a 60% decline in wild vertebrate populations between 1970 and 2014, ocean dead zones, megadroughts, glacier loss, wet-bulb temperatures past the survivability threshold, and then to notice that the proposal is to press the reset button and run it again.
- On **politics**, [MacAskill](/e/william-macaskill)'s constructive proposal is [**charter cities**](/e/charter-cities): autonomous communities with their own laws serving as laboratories for economic policy. "There could be Marxist [charter cities](/e/charter-cities) and environmental [charter cities](/e/charter-cities) and anarchist communitarian [charter cities](/e/charter-cities), and we could find out empirically which of these brings about the best society." The obvious objection is historical: Marxist and anarchist experiments have existed, and were embargoed, infiltrated, couped, and crushed by the prevailing global order. Charter cities, in practice, have been a libertarian project.
- Across a 300-page book about the long-term future, **capitalism and imperialism go essentially unexamined**, capitalism appears mainly in the title of someone else's book, and imperialism only in reference to long-dead empires.

[MacAskill](/e/william-macaskill) is, notably, among the *moderate* longtermists. That is the point at which critics stop arguing about premises and start arguing about what the framework is *for*.

## Part VII: The Bundle: TESCREAL

Seattle, Washington, 2023. [**Timnit Gebru**](/e/timnit-gebru) is at her screen. Born in Addis Ababa to Eritrean parents, she fled the Eritrean–Ethiopian war at fifteen, obtained asylum in the United States, and took a Stanford PhD in computer vision. In 2020, as co-lead of [Google](/e/google)'s ethical AI team, she co-authored **"[On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots](/e/stochastic-parrots)"**, a paper on the social risks of large language models, and was forced out of the company over it in December 2020. In December 2021 she founded the [**Distributed AI Research Institute**](/e/dair).

Now she had a larger target: mapping a network of ideologies shared by the most powerful people in Silicon Valley that the public could not even name.

Beside her was a philosopher who had lived inside it. **Émile P. Torres**, raised in a fundamentalist evangelical household, fascinated early by eschatology, later an atheist, spent roughly the first decade of their career as a transhumanist, longtermist and effective altruist, writing for the [Future of Life Institute](/e/future-of-life-institute). Then they turned against it, and were, by their own account, pushed out.

In 2023 the two coined a term that became a key for understanding the power structure of the technology industry, developed at length in **"[The TESCREAL Bundle](/e/tescreal-bundle-paper): Eugenics and the Promise of Utopia through Artificial General Intelligence,"** published in *First Monday* in April 2024.

[**TESCREAL**](/e/tescreal) is an acronym of seven ideologies. Their argument is that these are not independent schools of thought but an interlocking bundle, overlapping, sharing roots, feeding one another. You cannot understand today's AGI race, or the motivations behind it, without the map.

### T, Transhumanism

The backbone. The belief that humanity should transcend its biological limits through technology: longer life, the end of disease, brain–computer interfaces that expand cognition, and ultimately immortality.

The word was popularised in **1957** by the biologist [**Julian Huxley**](/e/julian-huxley), who wrote that *"the human species can, if it wishes, transcend itself, not just sporadically... but in its entirety, as humanity."* Man remaining man, but transcending himself.

And here is the fact [Gebru](/e/timnit-gebru) and [Torres](/e/emile-torres) will not let go of: **Julian [Huxley](/e/julian-huxley) was a committed eugenicist, and served as president of the British Eugenics Society from 1959 to 1962.** He wrote that deliberate eugenics would not be natural selection but *artificial* selection, "involving a conscious aim and a deliberate control of the mechanisms of heredity," and could therefore "operate much more rapidly and much less wastefully than natural selection."

Eugenics, for most of the twentieth century, meant improving the genetic quality of human populations through coercive, usually racist, sexist, or ableist, practices. Nazi Germany sterilised and murdered those deemed genetically undesirable. Before that, California forcibly sterilised at least 20,000 people in asylums and hospitals between 1909 and 1979. That is *negative* eugenics. *Positive* eugenics encouraged those with "desirable" genes to reproduce more.

The underlying assumption is that evolution is a process of **improvement** rather than mere adaptation to environment, and the unavoidable question is *who decides what counts as improved?* Historically: racial purity, intelligence, physical fitness.

[Transhumanism](/e/transhumanism), on [Torres](/e/emile-torres)'s reading, goes further than eugenics: it does not merely want to improve the human race but to **transcend it** and produce something radically superior. Hence their formulation, **transhumanism is eugenics on steroids.**

Modern transhumanist organisations reject the coercive twentieth-century programme and describe their position as "liberal eugenics" or "germinal choice," emphasising individual reproductive freedom. That distinction is real. Whether it survives contact with a world of radically unequal access to enhancement technologies is the substance of the dispute. If [Neuralink](/e/neuralink) someday radically enhances cognition, who can afford the implant?

### E, Extropianism

The first organised transhumanist movement, founded in the 1980s by the philosopher **Max More**. Kelly Hayes describes it as a form of pro-science super-optimism that seeks to fight **entropy**, the universal tendency of all things to fall apart. Extropians denounced "deathism" and "biological fundamentalism," and held that conquering death was not only possible but obligatory, in part through cryonic preservation pending future biomedical revival.

The movement attracted a striking range of Silicon Valley figures: [Robin Hanson](/e/robin-hanson), [Marvin Minsky](/e/marvin-minsky), [**Ray Kurzweil**](/e/ray-kurzweil), [Nick Bostrom](/e/nick-bostrom), Julian Assange, and several people rumoured to be Bitcoin's creator. It no longer exists formally, but it provided the space in which the radical ideas flourished, ideas that continue under [Peter Thiel](/e/peter-thiel), [Sam Altman](/e/sam-altman), Brian Johnson and others.

### S, Singularitarianism

The belief popularised by [**Ray Kurzweil**](/e/ray-kurzweil) in a coming [**technological singularity**](/e/singularitarianism), the point at which progress becomes unstoppable and incomprehensible to unaided human minds.

[Torres](/e/emile-torres) separates two readings. The first is about **rate**: imagine the change between 1925 and today compressed into a minute, then into a second. The second is the [**intelligence explosion**](/e/intelligence-explosion), driven by *recursive self-improvement*: once you have an AGI that performs as well as a human across every cognitive domain of interest, you can hand it the job of designing the next generation of AI. AGI builds AGI+, which builds AGI++, and within days, hours perhaps, you have superintelligence.

You will see this theory everywhere. The much-publicised [**AI 2027**](/e/ai-2027) scenario projects 330,000 superhuman AI researcher copies thinking at 57× human speed by 2027, and by 2035 a trillion superintelligent copies thinking at 10,000× human speed, generating $400 trillion a year at an $8 quadrillion valuation. It went viral through the *New York Times*, the BBC, and YouTube videos with over ten million views combined.

### C, Cosmism

The belief that humanity's destiny is to take to the stars and radically transform the universe itself. [**Ben Goertzel**](/e/ben-goertzel) 2010 *Cosmist Manifesto* makes the religious parallel explicit:

> *"Space-time engineering and future magic will permit achieving by scientific means most of the promises of religions and many amazing things no human religion ever dreamed. Eventually, we will be able to resurrect the dead by copying them to the future."*

[Elon Musk](/e/elon-musk)'s Mars vision is cosmism's most visible contemporary expression. Read as the expansionist endpoint of transhumanism, it becomes clear why the doctrine appeals to libertarian technologists: it promises limitless growth in a finite world by redefining the container.

### R, Rationalism

Not the rationalism of Descartes. This is the community built by the AI researcher [**Eliezer Yudkowsky**](/e/eliezer-yudkowsky) around the forum [**LessWrong**](/e/lesswrong) (founded 2009, following *Overcoming Bias* in 2006), joined later by [Scott Alexander](/e/scott-alexander)'s *Slate Star Codex* (2013). Rationalists aim to identify the biases in human thinking and make better decisions; the community is intensely focused on the potential dangers of AI. [Yudkowsky](/e/eliezer-yudkowsky)'s *Sequences* (2006–09) and his *Harry Potter and the Methods of Rationality* (2010–15) served as the on-ramp: a 2013 survey found a quarter of [LessWrong](/e/lesswrong) users arrived through the fanfiction.

[Yudkowsky](/e/eliezer-yudkowsky)'s own trajectory is the movement's spine. He began as a teenage extropian predicting that superintelligence would be a vast improvement over humans and might arrive by 2020. Two entrepreneurs in the extropian community bankrolled a think tank for him, the [**Singularity Institute for Artificial Intelligence**](/e/singularity-institute), later renamed the [**Machine Intelligence Research Institute**](/e/machine-intelligence-research-institute). Within months of starting the work he concluded he had been wrong, that smart did not automatically mean good, that AI could be a catastrophe, and that no fallback plan was possible. That reversal became his life's project: [**AI alignment**](/e/ai-alignment).

The consequences ran outward. [Yudkowsky](/e/eliezer-yudkowsky) courted [**Peter Thiel**](/e/peter-thiel) funding for the Singularity Institute; [Thiel](/e/peter-thiel) funded the **Singularity Summit** at Stanford; the most consequential product of that summit was the founding of [**DeepMind**](/e/deepmind), seeded by [Thiel](/e/peter-thiel) with $2.25 million. [Demis Hassabis](/e/demis-hassabis) described his ten-year plan for [DeepMind](/e/deepmind) as a "Manhattan Project for AI", a phrase [Altman](/e/sam-altman) would later repurpose. [Sam Altman](/e/sam-altman) has said [Yudkowsky](/e/eliezer-yudkowsky) "got many of us interested in AGI" and was "critical in the decision to start [OpenAI](/e/openai)." [Yudkowsky](/e/eliezer-yudkowsky)'s ideas shaped [Bostrom](/e/nick-bostrom)'s writing, which gave them academic credibility and institutional acceptance.

In a 2023 *TIME* op-ed [Yudkowsky](/e/eliezer-yudkowsky) wrote that *"the most likely result of building superhumanly smart AI is that literally everyone on Earth will die"*, not as a remote possibility but as the obvious default, and argued that enforcement of an international moratorium should extend to destroying a rogue datacentre by airstrike. He expanded the case in *[**If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies**](/e/if-anyone-builds-it-everyone-dies)* (with [Nate Soares](/e/nate-soares), September 2025).

Critics see something else in the same body of work. [Yudkowsky](/e/eliezer-yudkowsky)'s famous "[torture vs. dust specks](/e/torture-vs-dust-specks)" post argues that it is better for one person to be tortured for fifty years than for an unfathomably large number of people to each get a speck of dust in the eye for a moment, because the aggregate disutility of the specks is larger. To a critic, this is the whole method in miniature: everything reduces to numbers, utility, expected value; nothing is simply *wrong*; and the reduction is performed with total confidence by people who have often not read the relevant literature.

The community around these ideas has drawn serious criticism on its own terms. [Gideon Lewis-Kraus](/e/gideon-lewis-kraus) has argued that rationalism "gave safe harbour to some genuinely egregious ideas," including scientific racism and neoreactionary politics, [Curtis Yarvin](/e/curtis-yarvin)'s neoreaction found early audiences in these spaces. *Bloomberg* reported allegations from eight women of pervasive sexual misconduct in rationalist and EA circles that they say was frequently downplayed; *TIME* interviewed seven women describing harassment, coercion and assault, with one, [Sonia Joseph](/e/sonia-joseph), describing the culture as *"misogyny encoded into math."* Members and former members describe cult-like dynamics: communal houses, in-group dating norms, "doom circles" in which participants are told all the ways they might fail. And in 2022–2025 the community produced an actual violent offshoot, the [**Zizians**](/e/zizians), a group that emerged from rationalist circles after a falling-out with [MIRI](/e/machine-intelligence-research-institute) and [CFAR](/e/cfar), and that has been linked to six deaths.

**The steel-man:** many rationalists reject [Yudkowsky](/e/eliezer-yudkowsky)'s doom scenario entirely, the community is genuinely divided about him, and "some members of an online movement did terrible things" is not by itself an argument against its ideas. But the movement's own claim is that it is unusually good at reasoning, which makes its epistemic failures fair game.

### E and A, Effective Altruism and Longtermism

The final two letters are the ones we have already traced: the principle of doing the most good possible, and its evolved form focused on the long-term future.

### The core claim

[Gebru](/e/timnit-gebru) and [Torres](/e/emile-torres)'s argument is that these seven look like independent schools but share a common ancestor, and that the bundle carries historical and intellectual kinship with twentieth-century eugenics debates, an inheritance of reasoning that opens the door to treating some people's intelligence, or some people's visions of the future, as worth more than others'.

The people they name as involved in more than one of the [TESCREAL](/e/tescreal) ideologies include **Elon [Musk](/e/elon-musk), [Sam Altman](/e/sam-altman), [Peter Thiel](/e/peter-thiel), [Jaan Tallinn](/e/jaan-tallinn), [Dustin Moskovitz](/e/dustin-moskovitz), [Vitalik Buterin](/e/vitalik-buterin), Sam Bankman-Fried** and [**Marc Andreessen**](/e/marc-andreessen), who for a period described himself as a "TESCREALIST." Collectively these figures have moved tens of billions of dollars toward institutions, research funds, philosophers ([Bostrom](/e/nick-bostrom), [MacAskill](/e/william-macaskill)) and internet intellectuals ([Yudkowsky](/e/eliezer-yudkowsky)) in this space.

And here is the critical point. **This ideological frame is the main motivational source of the AGI race.** [OpenAI](/e/openai)'s mission to ensure that artificial general intelligence "benefits all of humanity"; [Anthropic](/e/anthropic)'s goal of building safe and steerable AI systems; [DeepMind](/e/deepmind)'s vision of "solving intelligence", these are different shades of [TESCREAL](/e/tescreal) commitments. In this frame AGI is not merely a technical objective but something close to a religious promise: **humanity's saviour, the solver of existential risk, the key to a cosmic future.**

[Gebru](/e/timnit-gebru)'s sharpest argument is that [TESCREAL](/e/tescreal) ideologies **use the language of existential threat to legitimise building dangerous and unscoped systems.** The fear that "AI could destroy humanity" becomes, paradoxically, the justification for pouring trillions into building AGI. *If we don't control it, someone else will* accelerates the arms race. And the cost of that race, today's concrete harms: algorithmic discrimination, environmental damage, labour exploitation, copyright violation, concentration of power, stays invisible in the shadow of a speculative future.

[Torres](/e/emile-torres) notes a structural consequence: the debate gets organised around a **utopia/apocalypse dichotomy**, two extremes that between them crowd out the present. Whether the machine brings paradise or extinction, the question of who is being harmed right now, and by whom, never gets asked.

### Taking the criticism of the criticism seriously

[TESCREAL](/e/tescreal) is a polemical instrument as well as an analytical one, and it has drawn substantive objections that belong in any honest account:

- **Overgeneralisation.** Critics argue that [Gebru](/e/timnit-gebru) and [Torres](/e/emile-torres) treat philosophies with mutually exclusive tenets as a single monolith. [Yudkowsky](/e/eliezer-yudkowsky)'s doomerism and [Andreessen](/e/marc-andreessen)'s accelerationism are not the same movement; they are in open conflict.
- **Mischaracterisation.** [Oliver Habryka](/e/oliver-habryka), among others, has said he has never encountered some of the groups described in the way they are described.
- **Selectivity.** [Ethan Zuckerman](/e/ethan-zuckerman) has argued that the framing itself risks reproducing what it criticises by keeping attention on the billionaires rather than on immediate harms such as racial inequity, algorithmic bias and environmental degradation.
- **Polemic over history.** [Seth Lazar](/e/seth-lazar) argues the term ignores many other strands in the history of AI that do not fit the narrative.

A reasonable position is that [TESCREAL](/e/tescreal) is best used as a **map of intellectual genealogy and funding networks**, not as an accusation that any individual believes all seven things. Read that way, the bundle explains something real: why a small group of people who disagree violently about whether AI will save or destroy us nevertheless agree, with remarkable unanimity, that building it as fast as possible is the correct response.

## Part VIII: Rival Faiths: e/acc and the Case for Growth

The ideology has a schism, and it clarifies what is actually being argued about.

[**Effective accelerationism**](/e/effective-accelerationism), formulated by [Guillaume Verdon](/e/guillaume-verdon) (writing as "[Beff Jezos](/e/guillaume-verdon)"), defines itself explicitly in opposition to [effective altruism](/e/effective-altruism). [Verdon](/e/guillaume-verdon)'s critique of EA is that it optimises a bad loss function. EA's objective is roughly hedonic, units of well-being, minus suffering, and, he argues, that objective has spurious minima: you can end up minimising shrimp-farming pain, or wireheading, or scrolling TikTok forever, feeling good on a short time horizon while decaying on a long one. e/acc proposes instead to measure the progress of civilisation thermodynamically: not by a subjective loss function but by energy capture and the expansion of complexity. *"You can believe in gravity or not, but it's not necessarily an option to obey it."*

His account of what went wrong with EA is worth quoting, because it comes from a rival rather than a critic on the left:

> It provided a sense of community for engineers and intellectuals and rationalists in the early days... but then they formed all sorts of organisations and started routing capital and having actual power. They influence the government, they influence most AI orgs... ultimately it was the perfect cover to acquire tons of power and capital, and unfortunately that corrupts people over time.

He also concedes the symmetry: the same critique applies to his own movement, and he invites the scepticism.

Fridman's counter-question to him is the one this whole essay circles: should we be sceptical of **the tyranny of an equation**, of reducing human civilisation to a loss function to optimise? [Verdon](/e/guillaume-verdon)'s answer is that his function is statistical rather than deterministic, a description of thermodynamic reality rather than a value imposed on it. That is precisely the move critics identify in every branch of the bundle: **presenting a contested value choice as a description of physical necessity.**

There is a third position, neither EA nor e/acc, that deserves a hearing because it is the mainstream one. The popular [Kurzgesagt](/e/kurzgesagt) explainer "A Selfish Argument for Making the World a Better Place" makes the case that we live in a [**positive-sum world**](/e/positive-sum-economics): since the Industrial Revolution, growth has turned the fixed pie into a growing one, and it is therefore in your narrow self-interest that everyone else prospers, because innovation follows supply and demand. Lift a billion people into a position to invent and to pay for inventions, and you multiply the research output aimed at the problems that will kill you. It is a genuinely powerful argument for global development, and it shares with the [TESCREAL](/e/tescreal) bundle the assumption that growth can continue indefinitely on a finite planet, which is exactly the assumption the critics dispute. It is also worth noting, in a discussion about who funds which stories, that the video was produced in partnership with Our World in Data and supported by the [Gates Foundation](/e/gates-foundation).

## Part IX: Where the Ideology Meets the Balance Sheet

### Anthropic

San Francisco, 2021. One evening a group of engineers quietly leaves [OpenAI](/e/openai)'s offices. Their leader is [**Dario Amodei**](/e/dario-amodei), for nearly five years [OpenAI](/e/openai)'s VP of research. With him is his sister [**Daniela Amodei**](/e/daniela-amodei), and in total seven co-founders: [Jared Kaplan](/e/jared-kaplan), [Jack Clark](/e/jack-clark), [Chris Olah](/e/chris-olah), [Ben Mann](/e/ben-mann), [Sam McCandlish](/e/sam-mccandlish) and [Tom Brown](/e/tom-brown). They are going to start their own company: [**Anthropic**](/e/anthropic), incorporated in January 2021, with the mission of ensuring that transformative AI benefits people and society.

The reason for the split looked simple. With billions arriving from [Microsoft](/e/microsoft), [OpenAI](/e/openai) was tilting from safety toward commercialisation. The [Amodeis](/e/dario-amodei) wanted safety at the core.

[Anthropic](/e/anthropic) was structured as a public benefit corporation and added an unusual governance mechanism: the [**Long-Term Benefit Trust**](/e/long-term-benefit-trust), whose job is to elect board members and keep the company from drifting off mission. Two names on the Trust drew attention: [**Paul Christiano**](/e/paul-christiano), founder of the [Alignment Research Center](/e/alignment-research-center), and [**Zach Robinson**](/e/zach-robinson), CEO of Effective Ventures US. Both had deep ties to the [effective altruism](/e/effective-altruism) community. The full founding slate, announced in September 2023, sat [Neil Buddy Shah](/e/neil-buddy-shah) (chair) alongside [Jason Matheny](/e/jason-matheny), [Christiano](/e/paul-christiano), [Kanika Bahl](/e/kanika-bahl) and [Robinson](/e/zach-robinson), of whom only Shah remained by early 2026, joined by [Richard Fontaine](/e/richard-fontaine), after Matheny's December 2023 exit, Christiano's move to the US AI Safety Institute in April 2024, and the January 2026 departures of Bahl and Robinson.

The real detonation was the funding. In April 2022, before [FTX](/e/ftx) collapsed, [Anthropic](/e/anthropic) received $580 million in funding including a **$500 million investment from FTX** under Bankman-Fried. In the bankruptcy process it emerged that much of this likely came from customer funds, that is, from stolen money. The irony was unbearable: the money of the man who did most to destroy the [effective altruism](/e/effective-altruism) movement had financed one of that movement's most important AI projects.

In March 2024 the [FTX](/e/ftx) estate sold its [Anthropic](/e/anthropic) stake for **$884 million**, providing partial repayment to creditors. People defrauded by [FTX](/e/ftx) recovered part of their money, ironically, through the appreciation of an AI company.

The network tightened further: [**Holden Karnofsky**](/e/holden-karnofsky), [GiveWell](/e/givewell) co-founder, [Open Philanthropy](/e/open-philanthropy) co-CEO, recipient of the early warnings about Bankman-Fried, left [Open Philanthropy](/e/open-philanthropy) in 2023, spent 2024 as a visiting scholar at the Carnegie Endowment, and in January 2025 joined [**Anthropic**](/e/anthropic), where his wife, [**Daniela Amodei**](/e/daniela-amodei), is president. Nothing improper follows from this. But a knowledge graph of who funds, governs, marries, warns and hires whom in this world is a very small, very dense graph, and that density is itself one of the critics' central claims.

### What "AI safety" came to mean

"[AI safety](/e/ai-safety)" sounds entirely innocent. Who doesn't want safe AI? But in the EA context the term acquired a specific and narrow meaning. It covered **hypothetical future existential risks**, the scenario in which an uncontrolled artificial general intelligence destroys humanity, and not today's concrete AI problems: algorithmic discrimination, data privacy violations, labour-market effects, environmental damage.

This distinction is critical, because while EA-influenced research funds directed hundreds of millions of dollars into long-term existential-risk research, researchers working on present-day harms struggled to find funding. [Yudkowsky](/e/eliezer-yudkowsky) has been explicit that he regards [AI ethics](/e/ai-ethics) work as a distraction from what really matters. The field's own vocabulary encodes the split: [**AI safety**](/e/ai-safety) (alignment, existential risk) versus [**AI ethics**](/e/ai-ethics) (bias, exploitation, environmental cost).

[80,000 Hours](/e/eighty-thousand-hours)' career recommendations show the result. Of its top-ranked pressing problems, most concern artificial intelligence. A striking omission from its top problem list is **climate change**, not existential enough, and not neglected enough, on the framework's own criteria.

### Five days in November

San Francisco, November 2023. The most dramatic five days in the AI world.

On Friday **17 November**, [OpenAI](/e/openai)'s non-profit board removed CEO [**Sam Altman**](/e/sam-altman). The official statement was vague: he had not been "consistently candid in his communications." Behind the curtain lay a deeper conflict.

Six people sat on [OpenAI](/e/openai)'s board. Some were publicly seen as close to [effective altruism](/e/effective-altruism) and [AI safety](/e/ai-safety) circles, the think-tank researcher [**Helen Toner**](/e/helen-toner) and [**Tasha McCauley**](/e/tasha-mccauley). [**Adam D'Angelo**](/e/adam-dangelo) was often described as closer to the safety camp in this dispute. Co-founder and chief scientist [**Ilya Sutskever**](/e/ilya-sutskever)'s views on AI risk were aligned with the EA line. But the real issue was that the board was split along a **speed-versus-safety** axis. Reporting indicated the board believed [Altman](/e/sam-altman) was commercialising AI too quickly and not taking safety concerns seriously enough. [Altman](/e/sam-altman) regarded that braking as timid and slow.

Silicon Valley rose up. [Microsoft](/e/microsoft), which had invested $10 billion (its cumulative commitment is generally reported at around $13 billion), was furious that the CEO of its partner had been fired overnight. **745 of [OpenAI](/e/openai)'s 770 employees** signed a letter saying they would move to [Microsoft](/e/microsoft) en masse unless the board resigned, [Sutskever](/e/ilya-sutskever) among them, publicly apologising and defecting. After five days of chaotic negotiation, lobbying and press warfare, [Altman](/e/sam-altman) returned on **22 November** and the board was reconstituted under [Bret Taylor](/e/bret-taylor), with the EA-linked members effectively removed.

For [effective altruism](/e/effective-altruism) those five days were a catastrophe. Shortly after the [FTX](/e/ftx) collapse, here again were the movement's fingerprints on a crisis, and again on the losing side.

The episode exposed structural flaws in EA's approach to [AI safety](/e/ai-safety):

1. **Disconnection from civil society.** There was almost no dialogue between researchers working on present harms and researchers working on existential risk.
2. **Lack of practical expertise.** EA was effective in forums and ineffective in boardrooms.
3. **Concentration of power.** The research agenda was being set not by any democratic process but by the philosophical preferences of a handful of billionaires.

And yet, against all these criticisms, EA's influence on [AI safety](/e/ai-safety) cannot be denied. Every major AI lab bears its imprint. [OpenAI](/e/openai) grew out of the existential-risk concerns of this community. [Anthropic](/e/anthropic) was founded by people from within it. [Google](/e/google) [DeepMind](/e/deepmind)'s safety research teams are full of researchers shaped by it. **The first people to take [AI safety](/e/ai-safety) seriously were members of this community.** However justified the criticisms, that historical fact does not go away.

## Part X: Oxford, 2026

Walk Oxford's stone courtyards today and you notice that the name once whispered in the corridors is now pronounced in a quieter, more cautious tone.

The [Centre for Effective Altruism](/e/centre-for-effective-altruism)'s CEO, [**Zach Robinson**](/e/zach-robinson), acknowledged the situation openly in a strategy document published in March 2025: [effective altruism](/e/effective-altruism) spent most of the preceding two years in a defensive crouch. It survived the predictions that [FTX](/e/ftx)'s collapse would bring EA down with it, but the momentum stopped.

Robinson's words were gentle; the reality was harsh. The Bankman-Fried scandal took not only the movement's money but its most valuable asset: **credibility**.

The financial loss was only the surface. The deeper damage was reputational. Months after the scandal, [**William MacAskill**](/e/william-macaskill) published a long post on the EA Forum containing a genuine retraction: *from now on, the fact that someone displays sincere-seeming moral signals, being vegan, say, or planning to give away most of their wealth, does not mean they have moral integrity in other areas.* One of the movement's founders was questioning one of its founding assumptions.

After [FTX](/e/ftx) the leaders went quiet. [**Dustin Moskovitz**](/e/dustin-moskovitz), the movement's largest living donor, deactivated his social media accounts for a period. [**Holden Karnofsky**](/e/holden-karnofsky) left [Open Philanthropy](/e/open-philanthropy). In 2024 Oxford closed the [**Future of Humanity Institute**](/e/future-of-humanity-institute), the intellectual home of [longtermism](/e/longtermism), after nineteen years. [Bostrom](/e/nick-bostrom) himself had, in January 2023, been forced to apologise for a 1996 message in which he had written that "Blacks are more stupid than whites" and used a racial slur, an apology that, for critics, confirmed rather than dispelled the concern about the genealogy.

### The ledger

The legacy of [effective altruism](/e/effective-altruism) is too complicated to compress into a sentence.

On one side there are children alive today because of bed nets. On the other there are the victims of an $8 billion fraud.

On one side there are researchers who took the risks of artificial intelligence seriously before anyone else. On the other there are people who used that concern as an excuse to ignore present-day harms, algorithmic discrimination, privacy violations, labour exploitation.

On one side there are more than ten thousand ordinary people who have pledged 10% of their income and kept the promise. On the other there is a funding structure without democratic oversight that hands decision-making power to a handful of billionaires.

### What to build instead

The critics are not only critics. [**Ruha Benjamin**](/e/ruha-benjamin), the sociologist of race, justice and technology, opens a talk with the observation that a small sliver of humanity is currently imposing its visions on the rest of us: they invest in space travel, superintelligence and underground bunkers while casting healthcare and housing for all as outlandish and unimaginable. These futurists let their imaginations run wild when it comes to bending material and digital reality, but their visions "grow limp" when it comes to transforming social reality so that everyone has a chance at a good life.

Benjamin argues that the utopia and apocalypse stories are two sides of one coin: in both, technology acts on us, and the human inputs, the values, assumptions, interests and needs that shape our digital and physical worlds, go missing. "To move forward, we have to pull back the screen." Borrowing a word from Margaret Atwood, she proposes [**ustopia**](/e/ustopia): "Utopias are the stuff of dreams, dystopias the stuff of nightmares; [ustopia](/e/ustopia) are what we create together when we're wide awake."

There are concrete versions of this. [**Decidim**](/e/decidim), the free open-source participatory democracy platform, is used by Barcelona, Helsinki and New York. [DAIR](/e/dair) runs a series called **Possible Futures**, "an imaginative space where near-future speculative storytelling meets grounded social critique", in which, for example, [**Nyalleng Moorosi**](/e/nyalleng-moorosi) asks what becomes possible when technology grows out of a deep understanding of community needs: Indigenous technologists reclaiming their languages from colonisation; tools that make existing informal food-trading networks more efficient, reducing waste and putting fresh local produce below supermarket prices, "all while working within familiar cultural frameworks rather than imposed foreign concepts of commerce."

Why is that so much harder to picture than a Mars colony? The activist [Rob Hopkins](/e/rob-hopkins) calls it a [**crisis of imagination**](/e/crisis-of-imagination), arriving precisely when we most need imagination: "Given everything that humanity has accomplished, all of it driven by leaps of imagination, why is envisioning a safer, saner, more peaceful path forward so consistently beyond our reach?" His sharper worry is that imagination has been captured: even among people working in creative industries, it "seems increasingly harnessed to create demand for things nobody really needs... almost as if imagination has been co-opted in the service of our own extinction."

And against the technologist's preference for a clean slate, Anthony Levandowski's "in technology, all that matters is tomorrow", there is James Baldwin: *"To accept one's past, one's history, is not the same thing as drowning in it; it is learning how to use it. An invented past can never be used. It cracks and crumbles under the pressures of life like clay in a season of drought."*

### The question

The question is still the same one [Singer](/e/peter-singer) asked beside a shallow pond in 1971.

**Will you see the [drowning child](/e/drowning-child)? And if you do, what will you do about it?**

The fifty years since have shown that the answer is not only a matter of arithmetic. The arithmetic is the easy part. The hard part is the thing the arithmetic keeps quietly deciding on your behalf: *whose* child, *when*, and *who gets to hold the calculator.*

## Appendix C: Corrections to the source material

Factual corrections made in this version:

1. **FTX Future Fund founding date**: the fund was created in **early 2022** (sources differ between a January 2022 establishment and a late-February 2022 public launch).
2. **Giving What We Can founders**: GWWC was founded by **Ord, MacAskill and Bernadette Young**.
3. **MacAskill's age in 2009**: born in 1987, he was **21-22** at GWWC's founding.
4. **SBF's MIT talk**: accounts vary between **2011 and 2012**. SBF was born in March 1992 and graduated MIT in 2014.
5. **Alameda Research founding**: founded **November 2017**.
6. **CZ's FTT sale**: reported at approximately **$529 million**, announced **6 November 2022**.
7. **Anthropic stake sale**: a **majority stake sold for $884 million** in March 2024.
8. **Anthropic founding**: **seven co-founders in total**, incorporated January 2021.
9. **OpenAI board**: the four board members who removed Altman were **Sutskever, D'Angelo, Toner and McCauley**; the employee letter had **745 of 770** signatories; Microsoft's investment at the time was **$10 billion**, with cumulative commitments reported around $13 billion.
10. **Name renderings**: rendered here as **Eliezer Yudkowsky, LessWrong, Ray Kurzweil, Nick Bostrom**.
11. **Open Philanthropy** was renamed **Coefficient Giving** in 2025.
