## Part I: The Drowning Child

<div class="section-image my-8 flex justify-center"><img src="/future-of-ai/images/sections/web/section-1.jpg" alt="" loading="lazy" decoding="async" width="256" height="256" class="w-64 h-64 rounded-lg object-cover border border-surface-2"></div>

Oxford, England, 1971. In the autumn, a twenty-five-year-old Australian philosopher is finishing his graduate work and starting his first teaching post. He is quiet, precise and not yet famous. On the news, millions of people are fleeing war and famine in East Bengal. His name is [Peter Singer](/e/peter-singer), and he sits down to write what looks like an ordinary journal article.

It was not ordinary. Published in 1972 in *Philosophy & Public Affairs* as **"[Famine, Affluence, and Morality](/e/famine-affluence-and-morality)"**, it posed one of the most uncomfortable moral questions of the twentieth century.

Singer sets a simple scene. You are walking past a shallow pond, and in it a small child is drowning. Nobody else is around. If you wade in, you ruin your clothes.

Would you let the child drown to stay clean?

Of course not. No sane person watches a child die to avoid some mud.

Then comes the second question, the one that detonates centuries of moral philosophy. Suppose the child is not in front of you but thousands of kilometres away, in a refugee camp on the border of East Bengal, and a small sum from you would keep them alive. Why is that money still in your pocket? **Does distance dissolve moral responsibility?**

The argument is disarmingly tight: *if it is in our power to prevent something very bad from happening, without thereby sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, we ought to do it.* Follow it through and you arrive somewhere radical. Nearly everyone living comfortably ought to be giving away a large share of their income, continuously, for life.

The reaction split along a line that has never closed. Some came to call Singer the most important moral philosopher alive. Others called him dangerous. Both camps agreed the logic was hard to escape.

> Is there a real moral difference between the child in front of you and the one you will never see? If you say yes, try to name it. If you say no, what follows for how you live?

It took forty years for the argument to become a movement, and a decade more for that movement to arrive at the centre of the most powerful technology of the century.

## Part II: Oxford, and the Question Singer Never Asked

<div class="section-image my-8 flex justify-center"><img src="/future-of-ai/images/sections/web/section-2.jpg" alt="" loading="lazy" decoding="async" width="256" height="256" class="w-64 h-64 rounded-lg object-cover border border-surface-2"></div>

Oxford again, 2006. A young philosopher named [Toby Ord](/e/toby-ord) is reading Singer. Australian family, modest and quiet, and intellectually enormously ambitious.

Ord did a calculation. On an academic's salary, over a working life, he could give away enough to save, by the best estimates available, thousands of lives.

But which charity should get it? This was the question Singer had not asked and Ord could not stop asking. **If you want to do good, how do you do the most good?** Charities are not equal. Some turn a dollar into years of healthy life; others, with the same money, produce almost nothing anyone can measure.

Ord pledged to give away everything he earned above a modest threshold. In **2009**, with fellow philosopher [William MacAskill](/e/william-macaskill) and physician [Bernadette Young](/e/bernadette-young), he founded [Giving What We Can](/e/give-what-we-can): twenty-three members at first, each pledging at least 10% of their income to the most effective organizations the evidence could find.

MacAskill, a Scot from Glasgow then in his early twenties, wanted the frame widened. Not only *how much should we give?* but *how should we live to do the most good?* Career choice, time, even diet belonged in the calculation. In **2011** he and [Benjamin Todd](/e/benjamin-todd) founded [80,000 Hours](/e/eighty-thousand-hours) (roughly the number of working hours in a career), and the movement gathering around these ideas chose its name: [effective altruism](/e/effective-altruism). In **2012** the [Centre for Effective Altruism](/e/centre-for-effective-altruism) was incorporated at Oxford as its umbrella body.

Hidden in the seed of the movement, though, was code that would grow into an enormous forest or, depending on your view, an enormous fire. Inside the phrase **"do the most good"** sit three unanswered questions:

- **Good for whom?**
- **Over what time horizon?**
- **And decided by whom?**

Within fifteen years those three questions would gather Silicon Valley's brightest minds, largest fortunes and most dangerous ideas around a single movement.

## Part III: The Measurable Good

<div class="section-image my-8 flex justify-center"><img src="/future-of-ai/images/sections/web/section-3.jpg" alt="" loading="lazy" decoding="async" width="256" height="256" class="w-64 h-64 rounded-lg object-cover border border-surface-2"></div>

It's 2007. Two young analysts at the hedge fund Bridgewater Associates, [Elie Hassenfeld](/e/elie-hassenfeld) and [Holden Karnofsky](/e/holden-karnofsky), notice that charity lives in a dark age of transparency. In finance, every investment is dissected. In philanthropy, almost nothing is. They leave to found [GiveWell](/e/givewell), which judges charities not by overhead ratios or glossy reports but by evidence of outcomes.

GiveWell became the movement's reference point. Its recommendations are stubbornly unglamorous: insecticide-treated bed nets from the [Against Malaria Foundation](/e/against-malaria-foundation), vitamin A supplements, small cash incentives for childhood vaccination. By its own estimates, a few thousand dollars to its top charities averts the death of a child which is an approximation, GiveWell insists, not a price tag.

In 2014 Karnofsky co-founded [Open Philanthropy](/e/open-philanthropy), funded by [Good Ventures](/e/good-ventures), the foundation of Facebook co-founder [Dustin Moskovitz](/e/dustin-moskovitz) and [Cari Tuna](/e/cari-tuna), the movement's largest living donors. It has since directed more than **$4 billion**. (In November 2025 it renamed itself Coefficient Giving.)

This is the part that is genuinely hard to argue with. Singer's pond had become a working mechanism. Ordinary people, skipping a coffee, were keeping alive children they would never meet.

### The objection from the other side of the transaction

And yet the critique that cuts deepest does not come from philosophers. It comes from the people on the receiving end.

[Anthony Kalulu](/e/anthony-kalulu), a farmer and community organizer from Busoga, Uganda's poorest region, has written about his disillusionment with EA's promise to the ultra-poor. Traditional philanthropy, he argues, keeps people in poverty as passive recipients; barely 1% of global anti-poverty funding reaches grassroots organizations in the Global South at all. EA sounded like the corrective. He found it worse: *"traditional philanthropy and EA alike have only pushed their own predetermined solutions, which aren't rooted in the lived experiences of the extreme poor."* And GiveWell's top charities are Western-led organizations working in Africa, not African-led ones.

[Émile P. Torres](/e/emile-torres), a former effective altruist, makes the structural version of the point: philanthropy transfers **wealth** from North to South while keeping the **power** to decide what counts, where the money goes and how it is used. Critics call the wider apparatus the [nonprofit industrial complex](/e/nonprofit-industrial-complex).

The essay collection [The Good It Promises, the Harm It Does](/e/good-it-promises-harm-it-does) (2023) turns this into a claim about knowledge. "Effectiveness" gets filtered through one contested method, [expected utility](/e/expected-utility), which then presents itself as simply what reason and evidence say, and decisions are handed to experts whose judgments are treated as value-free. In her foreword, [Amia Srinivasan](/e/amia-srinivasan) notes that EA has found almost nothing worth learning from the long movements for the rights of workers, colonized peoples, women, disabled and incarcerated people, treating them at best as *ineffective* ways of doing good, "not political formations from which to learn". Her verdict: EA "promises us a better world while implicitly encouraging us to accept the world more or less as it is."

**The defenders' answer** deserves stating in full. Bed nets work. Vitamin A works. Cash works. A movement that moved billions toward the interventions with the strongest evidence has a record its blind spots do not erase. And "fund grassroots organizing instead" is a proposal whose effects are much harder to measure, which is precisely the disagreement, not a rebuttal of it.

What neither side can settle with data is the question underneath: who gets to define "effective", a spreadsheet in San Francisco or a farmer in Busoga. It is the same question the AI industry now faces at world scale.

## Part IV: Earning to Give, and Eleven Days in November

<div class="section-image my-8 flex justify-center"><img src="/future-of-ai/images/sections/web/section-4.jpg" alt="" loading="lazy" decoding="async" width="256" height="256" class="w-64 h-64 rounded-lg object-cover border border-surface-2"></div>

Cambridge, Massachusetts, around 2012. At MIT there is a curly-haired physics student who lives in shorts and t-shirts and runs everything, his own life included, through cost-benefit analysis. His name is [Sam Bankman-Fried](/e/sam-bankman-fried), and both his parents are Stanford law professors. A visiting speaker, [William MacAskill](/e/william-macaskill), tells the students that **your career is the biggest moral decision of your life**. If you are strong at maths, you may do far more good *not* working at a charity. Go into finance. Earn as much as you can. Give most of it away.

The strategy had a name: [earning to give](/e/earning-to-give). It rested on [replaceability](/e/replaceability): your seat at a charity will be filled by someone about as good, but your seat at a trading firm goes to someone who won't donate. Bankman-Fried later said the argument redirected him from working on animal welfare. He joined [Jane Street](/e/jane-street), donated much of his salary, and became the movement's model citizen.

The doctrine also produced a certain kind of conversation. In one widely retold story, an EA-minded student told a law student she should not become a public defender: a corporate salary donated to malaria charities would save more lives, and her seat would be filled anyway. When she got angry, he took her anger as proof she couldn't handle impartial logic.

That shape recurs at every scale of this story. A chain of individually plausible steps, a conclusion ordinary moral intuition rejects, and the rejection treated as evidence of irrationality.

After a short stint at the Centre for Effective Altruism, Bankman-Fried founded [Alameda Research](/e/alameda-research), a crypto trading firm, in November 2017, and in 2019 the exchange [FTX](/e/ftx). At its peak his fortune was estimated at over $20 billion. He gave to politicians, to pandemic prevention and to AI safety, and in February 2022 launched the [FTX Future Fund](/e/ftx-future-fund), which committed about **$160 million** within months, almost all of it to long-term causes, the Centre for Effective Altruism and [Longview Philanthropy](/e/longview-philanthropy) among them. On the Fund's advisory team sat MacAskill; its CEO was the philosopher [Nick Beckstead](/e/nick-beckstead). FTX also led a **$500 million** investment in a young AI company called [Anthropic](/e/anthropic).

The warnings had come early. In 2018, about half of Alameda's staff walked out. According to TIME's March 2023 investigation, departing staff, including co-founder [Tara Mac Aulay](/e/tara-mac-aulay), told EA leaders that Bankman-Fried lied, mixed personal and company money and refused basic controls. "Sam will lie, and distort the truth for his own gain," one internal document said. The warnings reached MacAskill, Beckstead and Karnofsky ([the 2018 split](/e/alameda-2018-split)). They were treated as a "he said-she said" squabble. **Nobody hit the brakes.**

### Eleven days

- **2 November 2022.** *CoinDesk* publishes a leaked Alameda balance sheet. Most of its assets are FTT, a token FTX itself created.
- **6 November.** [Changpeng Zhao](/e/changpeng-zhao) says [Binance](/e/binance) will sell about $529 million of FTT. A bank run begins.
- **8 November.** A Binance rescue is announced; Zhao looks at the books and walks away.
- **10 November.** The Future Fund team resigns, citing "fundamental questions about the legitimacy and integrity" of the business behind it.
- **11 November.** FTX files for bankruptcy with roughly **$8 billion** of customer money missing ([collapse](/e/ftx-collapse)).

A year later Bankman-Fried was convicted on all seven counts, and he was sentenced to **25 years** in March 2024 ([trial](/e/us-v-bankman-fried)). Days after the collapse, he had told a *Vox* reporter that his "ethics stuff" was "mostly a front… a dumb game we woke westerners play where we say all the right shibboleths and so everyone likes us" ([the DMs](/e/sbf-vox-interview)).

Two readings compete. MacAskill called it a betrayal of EA's values ("If that goodwill laundered fraud, I am ashamed") and blamed hubris. Torres reads it as the framework working as designed: on a strict utilitarian, longtermist view nothing is wrong in itself, so a little fraud for an astronomically good future can look not merely allowed but obligatory ([ends justify the means](/e/ends-justify-means)). Which reading you accept decides whether the lesson is about one man or about the ideas.

There is a third lesson. The people who received the warnings were the same people whose projects the money funded, and nobody outside that circle had standing to act on what the insiders knew. That is a governance failure before it is a moral one.

The final irony came in 2024. The bankruptcy estate [sold](/e/ftx-anthropic-stake-sale) FTX's Anthropic shares for about $1.3 billion, and the defrauded customers were repaid in part by the rise of an AI company.

## Part V: Longtermism: From Today's Poor to Tomorrow's Trillions

<div class="section-image my-8 flex justify-center"><img src="/future-of-ai/images/sections/web/section-5.jpg" alt="" loading="lazy" decoding="async" width="256" height="256" class="w-64 h-64 rounded-lg object-cover border border-surface-2"></div>

While the bed nets were doing their measurable good, something larger and stranger was growing in Oxford. The question *whom do we save today?* was giving way to one much bigger, more abstract and more dangerous: **what do we owe the trillions of people who might be born in the future?**

### The philosophical ancestor

In **1984** the Oxford philosopher [Derek Parfit](/e/derek-parfit) published [Reasons and Persons](/e/reasons-and-persons). One of his questions: do we have obligations to people who don't exist yet? Parfit said yes, and argued it with nuclear waste. Bury it carelessly and, centuries from now, leaking radiation harms people not yet born. That harm is no less wrong because the victims don't exist yet. He also found two puzzles that would haunt everything built on his work: the [non-identity problem](/e/non-identity-problem) and the [repugnant conclusion](/e/repugnant-conclusion).

Most people agree with the nuclear-waste point. The next step is where the disagreement begins: *if future people matter, and there could be astronomically many of them, then the long-run effects of our actions may matter far more than the short-run ones.*

### The machinery

Around **2017**, Ord and MacAskill coined the word [longtermism](/e/longtermism).

In **2020** Ord published [The Precipice](/e/the-precipice), estimating roughly a **one-in-six** chance that humanity suffers an [existential catastrophe](/e/existential-risk) this century. In his breakdown the natural risks are trivial. The dangerous ones are all our own: unaligned AI first, engineered pandemics second.

The work was done at Oxford's [Future of Humanity Institute](/e/future-of-humanity-institute), founded in 2005 by the Swedish philosopher [Nick Bostrom](/e/nick-bostrom). His [Superintelligence](/e/superintelligence) (2014) treated AI-driven extinction with academic seriousness and hit Silicon Valley like an earthquake. After reading it, [Elon Musk](/e/elon-musk) began calling AI humanity's biggest existential threat.

In **2022**, at the height of FTX, MacAskill published [What We Owe the Future](/e/what-we-owe-the-future). Its three premises: future people matter as much as we do; there could be vast numbers of them; and what we do now can shape whether and how they live. Conclusion: **positively influencing the long-term future is a key moral priority of our time.**

### The arithmetic, and where it leads

The numbers are the argument. In [a 2021 paper](/e/strong-longtermism-2021), MacAskill and [Hilary Greaves](/e/hilary-greaves) argue that any reasonable estimate of the expected number of future beings is at least **10²⁴**. On that estimate, they write, "a reduction in near-term risk of extinction by one millionth of one percentage point" is worth "ten quadrillion (10¹⁶) lives saved." Bostrom's [astronomical waste](/e/astronomical-waste) argument (2003) goes further: every century we delay settling the cosmos forfeits roughly **10³⁸** potential lives. Counting digital minds, he reaches **10⁵⁸**.

Against numbers like these, a billion people saved from famine is a rounding error. That is not a rhetorical exaggeration. It is the literal structure of the calculation, and it points priority, mathematically, at extinction risk: runaway AI above all.

> A stranger stops you: "Give me your wallet, and I will create 10⁵⁰ happy lives. The chance I'm telling the truth is tiny, but surely not *that* tiny." Expected-value reasoning says pay him. Philosophers call this [Pascal's mugging](/e/fanaticism). Where exactly is the difference between the mugger and a funding pitch that promises a tiny reduction in extinction risk?

Silicon Valley clustered around the idea. [Sam Altman](/e/sam-altman), [Peter Thiel](/e/peter-thiel), [Jaan Tallinn](/e/jaan-tallinn), [Vitalik Buterin](/e/vitalik-buterin) and [Dustin Moskovitz](/e/dustin-moskovitz) all moved in its orbit, and Musk called *What We Owe the Future* "a close match for my philosophy". Longtermism was no longer a seminar topic. It was an investment thesis, and its first target was AI.

### Four objections

**1. You cannot predict the future.** What could someone in 1500 have done to improve the world of 2026? Almost nothing they could have foreseen. The critic B.V.E. Hyde adds that longtermism assumes a butterfly-effect view of history that overrates individual agency and underrates structural forces.

**2. Today's emergencies get crowded out.** As EA-influenced budgets shifted toward AI and existential risk, global health's share shrank in relative terms. The philosopher Elizabeth Hupfer calls this the "far-future priority objection".

**3. The ends justify the means.** If trillions of lives are at stake, what present wrong is ruled out? Bankman-Fried is the case critics cite, and the answer is not reassuring.

**4. The genealogy.** The most uncomfortable objection: that longtermism shares intellectual DNA with techno-utopianism and, in some respects, with the eugenics movement, the idea that some people's capacities, or some people's visions of the future, count for more.

### The tells

Some specific commitments make critics reach for the word "ideology" rather than "philosophy". They deserve to be quoted exactly, because they are often quoted badly.

- **Nick Beckstead**, who later ran the FTX Future Fund, wrote in his [2013 dissertation](/e/beckstead-dissertation-2013) that "saving lives in poor countries may have significantly smaller ripple effects than saving and improving lives in rich countries" because "richer countries have substantially more innovation, and their workers are much more economically productive." He concluded, with hedges, that "it now seems more plausible to me that saving a life in a rich country is substantially more important than saving a life in a poor country, other things being equal." The hedges are real. So is the direction of the reasoning.
- **On nature**, MacAskill defines EA as ["tentatively welfarist"](/e/welfarism): its aim "concerns promoting well-being only and not, say, protecting biodiversity or conserving natural beauty for their own sakes." Follow that further and you meet the worry that wild animals' lives contain more suffering than joy, so the loss of wild habitat might even be net positive ([wild-animal suffering](/e/wild-animal-suffering)).
- **On climate**, *What We Owe the Future* considers warming of 7 to 9.5 °C and finds it "hard to see how even this could lead directly to civilizational collapse". Part of the case for decarbonizing is to leave accessible fossil fuels for a possible re-industrialization after a collapse. Torres asks readers to recall what the first industrial revolution did to the living world, and to notice that the proposal is to keep the option of pressing reset and running it again.
- **On politics**, MacAskill's constructive idea is [charter cities](/e/charter-cities): autonomous zones as laboratories for different kinds of society. Critics answer from history. Socialist and anarchist experiments have existed, and were blockaded, infiltrated or crushed, while charter cities in practice have been a libertarian project. In a book about the long-term future, capitalism and imperialism go largely unexamined.

MacAskill is, notably, among the *moderate* longtermists. That is the point at which critics stop arguing about premises and start arguing about what the framework is *for*, and about who it puts in charge.

## Part VI: The Bundle: TESCREAL

<div class="section-image my-8 flex justify-center"><img src="/future-of-ai/images/sections/web/section-6.jpg" alt="" loading="lazy" decoding="async" width="256" height="256" class="w-64 h-64 rounded-lg object-cover border border-surface-2"></div>

[Timnit Gebru](/e/timnit-gebru) was born in Addis Ababa to Eritrean parents, fled war as a teenager, and earned a Stanford PhD in computer vision. In 2020, as co-lead of Google's Ethical AI team, she co-wrote a paper on the risks of large language models, ["On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots"](/e/stochastic-parrots), and Google forced her out that December ([her exit](/e/gebru-google-exit)). A year later she founded the [Distributed AI Research Institute](/e/dair). Now she had a larger target: a network of ideas shared by the most powerful people in the technology industry that the public had no name for.

Beside her was a philosopher who had lived inside it. [Émile P. Torres](/e/emile-torres), raised in an evangelical household fascinated by the end times, spent about a decade as a transhumanist, longtermist and effective altruist, writing for the [Future of Life Institute](/e/future-of-life-institute), before turning critic and, by their own account, being pushed out.

In 2023 the two coined a term, developed at length in ["**The TESCREAL Bundle**: Eugenics and the Promise of Utopia through Artificial General Intelligence"](/e/tescreal-bundle-paper), published in *First Monday* in April 2024. [TESCREAL](/e/tescreal) is an acronym of seven ideologies, and the claim is that they are not independent schools of thought but a bundle: overlapping, sharing roots, feeding one another. You cannot understand the AGI race, the argument goes, without the map.

### T is for Transhumanism

The backbone: humanity should transcend its biological limits through technology. Longer life, the end of disease, brain–computer interfaces, eventually a [posthuman](/e/posthuman) future.

The word was popularized in **1957** by the biologist [Julian Huxley](/e/julian-huxley): *"the human species can, if it wishes, transcend itself… in its entirety, as humanity."* And here is the fact Gebru and Torres will not let go of: **Huxley was a committed eugenicist, president of the British Eugenics Society from 1959 to 1962.** Deliberate eugenics, he wrote, would be *artificial* selection, which could "operate much more rapidly and much less wastefully than natural selection."

[Eugenics](/e/eugenics) meant improving populations by coercion. California forcibly sterilized some 20,000 people between 1909 and 1979; Nazi Germany sterilized and murdered those it called unfit. Underneath lay the assumption that evolution means *improvement*, and a question nobody has ever answered fairly: **improved by whose measure?**

[Transhumanism](/e/transhumanism), on Torres's reading, goes further still: not improving the species but replacing it with something superior. Hence their formulation, **"eugenics on steroids"**. Transhumanists reject coercion and call their view [liberal eugenics](/e/liberal-eugenics): individual choice, not state programs. That distinction is real. Whether it survives a world of radically unequal access is the dispute. If an implant could make you permanently smarter, who gets it first, and what does their children's advantage do to everyone else's?

### E is for Extropianism

The first organized transhumanist movement, launched in **1988** by the philosopher [Max More](/e/max-more). The organizer and podcaster [Kelly Hayes](/e/kelly-hayes) describes it as pro-science super-optimism at war with **entropy**, the tendency of everything to fall apart. [Extropians](/e/extropianism) denounced "deathism"; many signed up for cryonic preservation at [Alcor](/e/alcor), which More later ran. Its mailing list was the seedbed. [Robin Hanson](/e/robin-hanson), [Nick Bostrom](/e/nick-bostrom) and a teenage [Eliezer Yudkowsky](/e/eliezer-yudkowsky) posted there; [Ray Kurzweil](/e/ray-kurzweil) and [Marvin Minsky](/e/marvin-minsky) moved in the milieu; so did several of the cryptographers later rumoured to be Bitcoin's creator. The movement no longer exists. Its people and its vocabulary do.

### S is for Singularitarianism

The belief, popularized by [Ray Kurzweil](/e/ray-kurzweil), in a coming **technological singularity**, when progress becomes too fast for unaided humans to follow. Torres separates two versions. One is about *rate*: compress the change between 1925 and today into a minute, then a second. The other is the [intelligence explosion](/e/intelligence-explosion): an AI as good as a human at AI research designs its successor, AGI builds AGI+ builds AGI++, and within days you have superintelligence.

You will see this theory everywhere now. The 2025 scenario [AI 2027](/e/ai-2027), by the former OpenAI researcher [Daniel Kokotajlo](/e/daniel-kokotajlo) with [Scott Alexander](/e/scott-alexander), has AI automating its own research into superintelligence before the decade is out; it went viral through the *New York Times*, the BBC and YouTube. Leopold Aschenbrenner's [Situational Awareness](/e/situational-awareness) (2024) recast the same timeline as a US–China security race.

### C is for Cosmism

The belief that humanity's destiny is to spread through the cosmos and remake it. It begins with the nineteenth-century Russian [Nikolai Fedorov](/e/nikolai-fedorov), who taught that humanity's "common task" was to defeat death, resurrect every ancestor and settle the stars. In 2010 [Ben Goertzel](/e/ben-goertzel)'s [Cosmist Manifesto](/e/cosmist-manifesto) made the religious parallel explicit:

> Space-time engineering and future magic will permit achieving by scientific means most of the promises of religions, and many amazing things no human religion ever dreamed. Eventually we will be able to resurrect the dead by copying them to the future.

Musk's plan to make humanity multiplanetary through [SpaceX](/e/spacex) is [cosmism](/e/cosmism)'s most visible contemporary expression. It also explains the doctrine's appeal to technologists: it promises limitless growth on a finite planet by redefining the size of the container.

### R is for Rationalism

Not Descartes. This is the community built by [Eliezer Yudkowsky](/e/eliezer-yudkowsky) around [LessWrong](/e/lesswrong) (2009), which grew out of the blog *Overcoming Bias* (2006), joined later by Scott Alexander's *Slate Star Codex* (2013). [Rationalists](/e/rationalism) aim to identify the biases in human thinking and decide better, and the community is intensely focused on AI. Yudkowsky's [Sequences](/e/sequences) and his fanfiction [Harry Potter and the Methods of Rationality](/e/hpmor) were the on-ramps; in a 2013 survey about a quarter of LessWrong users said they arrived through the fanfiction.

Yudkowsky's own trajectory is the movement's spine. He began as a teenage extropian who expected superintelligence to be wonderful, and soon; extropian donors funded his [Singularity Institute](/e/singularity-institute) to build it. Within a few years he concluded he had been wrong. Smart does not mean good, and a superintelligence could be a catastrophe. That reversal became his life's work: [AI alignment](/e/ai-alignment).

The consequences ran outward. [Peter Thiel](/e/peter-thiel) funded the Singularity Institute and its [Singularity Summit](/e/singularity-summit). After the 2010 summit, [Demis Hassabis](/e/demis-hassabis) won a meeting with Thiel by opening with chess, and Thiel became the first major investor in [DeepMind](/e/deepmind) (reportedly about $2.25 million). In February 2023 Sam Altman wrote:

> eliezer has IMO done more to accelerate AGI than anyone else. certainly he got many of us interested in AGI, helped deepmind get funded at a time when AGI was extremely outside the overton window, was critical in the decision to start openai, etc.

A month later, in [TIME](/e/yudkowsky-time-oped-2023), Yudkowsky wrote that "the most likely result of building a superhumanly smart AI, *under anything remotely like the current circumstances*, is that literally everyone on Earth will die", and that a global moratorium should be enforced even by airstrikes on rogue data centres. He expanded the case in [If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies](/e/if-anyone-builds-it-everyone-dies) (2025, with Nate Soares).

> The loudest warning about AI helped recruit the founders of the labs that build it. What would a warning have to look like *not* to work as an advertisement?

Critics see something else in the same body of work. In ["Torture vs. Dust Specks"](/e/torture-vs-dust-specks) (2007), Yudkowsky argues it is better for one person to be tortured for fifty years than for 3^^^3 people (a number too large to write out) to each get a dust speck in the eye, because the specks add up. To a critic, that is the whole method in miniature: everything reduces to numbers, and nothing is simply wrong.

The community has also drawn serious criticism on its own terms. [Gideon Lewis-Kraus](/e/gideon-lewis-kraus) argues rationalism "gave safe harbour to some genuinely egregious ideas", including scientific racism and the neoreaction of [Curtis Yarvin](/e/curtis-yarvin). In 2023 *TIME* reported seven women's accounts of harassment and assault in EA circles (one, [Sonia Joseph](/e/sonia-joseph), called it "misogyny encoded into math"), and *Bloomberg* reported eight women's accounts across the rationalist and EA scene. And a small group from its Bay Area fringe, the [Zizians](/e/zizians), is linked to six deaths in California, Pennsylvania and Vermont between 2022 and 2025; prosecutions were still under way in 2026.

**The steel-man:** many rationalists reject Yudkowsky's doom, and "some members of an online movement did terrible things" is not an argument against its ideas. But the movement's claim to be unusually good at reasoning makes its reasoning failures fair game.

### E and A, and L: Effective Altruism and Longtermism

The last letters are the ones we have already traced: doing the most good possible, and its evolved form focused on the far future.

### The core claim

Gebru and Torres argue that these seven look independent but share a common ancestor, and carry an inheritance from twentieth-century eugenics: a habit of reasoning that treats some people's intelligence, or some people's visions of the future, as worth more than others'.

The people they name as involved in more than one of the ideologies include **Elon Musk, Sam Altman, Peter Thiel, Jaan Tallinn, Dustin Moskovitz, Vitalik Buterin** and **Sam Bankman-Fried**. [Marc Andreessen](/e/marc-andreessen) took the label as a badge: for several weeks in 2023 his X bio read "TESCREAList".

The critical point: **this frame, they argue, is the motivational engine of the AGI race.** OpenAI's mission to ensure AGI "benefits all of humanity", Anthropic's aim to build safe and steerable systems, DeepMind's goal to "solve intelligence" are different shades of the same commitment. AGI here is not a product. It is closer to a religious promise: **humanity's saviour, the solver of existential risk, the key to a cosmic future.**

Gebru's sharpest argument is a paradox. TESCREAL ideas **use the language of existential threat to legitimize building dangerous, unscoped systems**. The fear that AI could destroy humanity becomes the reason to pour hundreds of billions into building it, because *if we don't, someone else will* ([the AGI race](/e/ai-race)). Torres adds that the debate is then trapped in a [utopia/apocalypse binary](/e/utopia-apocalypse-binary): whether the machine brings paradise or extinction, nobody asks who is being harmed now, and by whom.

### The hidden faith

The title of this essay is not only a metaphor. Scholars of religion have argued for decades that technological futurism reuses religious structure ([techno-millenarianism](/e/techno-millenarianism)). The historian [David Noble](/e/david-noble) traced Western engineering to a dream of redemption in [The Religion of Technology](/e/religion-of-technology) (1997); [Robert Geraci](/e/robert-geraci)'s [Apocalyptic AI](/e/apocalyptic-ai) (2010) found AI futurists reproducing apocalyptic theology almost point for point, a fallen present and a coming rupture and a transcendent digital afterlife; and the anthropologist [Beth Singler](/e/beth-singler) studies how AI stories take the shapes of blessing, apocalypse and deification.

The bundle has an **eschatology** (the singularity, or extinction), a **salvation** (alignment, upload, the stars), **saints and prophets** (the manifesto literally lists "patron saints"), and a **priesthood**: the few labs and funders who understand the stakes and must therefore be trusted to act. Sometimes it is explicit. The engineer [Anthony Levandowski](/e/anthony-levandowski) registered a church, [Way of the Future](/e/way-of-the-future), devoted to "the realization of a Godhead based on Artificial Intelligence". Mostly it is implicit, and that is what makes it powerful. A faith that doesn't know it is one is hard to argue with, and harder still to vote on.

### Taking the criticism of the criticism seriously

TESCREAL is a polemical instrument as well as an analytical one, and it has drawn substantive objections:

- **Overgeneralization.** [Ozy Brennan](/e/ozy-brennan) and others argue it treats philosophies with opposed tenets as a monolith. Yudkowsky's doomerism and Andreessen's accelerationism are in open war.
- **Mischaracterization.** [Oliver Habryka](/e/oliver-habryka), who runs LessWrong, put it drily: "I've never in my life met a cosmist; apparently I'm great friends with them."
- **Selectivity.** [Ethan Zuckerman](/e/ethan-zuckerman) argues the framing keeps attention on billionaires instead of immediate harms like racial inequity, algorithmic bias and environmental damage.
- **Polemic over history.** [Seth Lazar](/e/seth-lazar) says the term ignores many strands of AI history that don't fit.

A reasonable position: TESCREAL is best used as a **map of intellectual genealogy and funding networks**, not as an accusation that anyone believes all seven things. Read that way it explains something real. A small group of people who disagree violently about whether AI will save or destroy us nevertheless agree, with remarkable unanimity, that it is the most important thing in history, that it should be built, or controlled, by people like them, and that there is no time to ask anyone else.

## Part VII: Rival Faiths: e/acc, d/acc and the Case for Growth

<div class="section-image my-8 flex justify-center"><img src="/future-of-ai/images/sections/web/section-7.jpg" alt="" loading="lazy" decoding="async" width="256" height="256" class="w-64 h-64 rounded-lg object-cover border border-surface-2"></div>

The bundle has a schism, and it clarifies what is really being argued.

[Effective accelerationism](/e/effective-accelerationism) (e/acc), formulated in 2022 by the physicist [Guillaume Verdon](/e/guillaume-verdon) writing as "Beff Jezos", defines itself against effective altruism. Verdon's critique is that EA optimizes a bad loss function. Its hedonic objective, units of well-being minus suffering, has spurious minima: you can end up minimizing shrimp-farm pain, or wireheading humanity into bliss, feeling good in the short run while decaying in the long one. e/acc proposes to measure civilization thermodynamically, by energy captured and complexity grown. "You can believe in gravity or not, but it's not necessarily an option to obey it."

His account of what went wrong with EA is worth quoting, because it comes from a rival, not a critic on the left:

> It provided a sense of community for engineers and intellectuals and rationalists in the early days… but then they formed all sorts of organizations and started routing capital and having actual power. They influence the government, they influence most AI orgs… ultimately it was the perfect cover to acquire tons of power and capital, and unfortunately that corrupts people over time.

He concedes the same critique applies to his own movement. Lex Fridman's counter-question is the one this essay keeps circling: should we be sceptical of **the tyranny of an equation**? Verdon answers that his function describes thermodynamic reality rather than imposing a value. Critics hear the move they find in every branch of the bundle: **a contested value choice presented as physical necessity**, which leaves nobody any room to decide otherwise.

Andreessen's [Techno-Optimist Manifesto](/e/techno-optimist-manifesto) (October 2023) gave the accelerationists a text. It argues that technology solves material problems, that energy use should rise a thousandfold, and that slowing AI costs lives. It lists "existential risk", "sustainability" and "tech ethics" among its enemies.

Between the poles, [Vitalik Buterin](/e/vitalik-buterin) proposed [d/acc](/e/d-acc) (November 2023): accelerate, but deliberately favor defensive, decentralized, democratic technologies over ones that concentrate power. It is one of the few ideas from inside the bundle that treats *who decides* as part of the engineering problem.

There is also a mainstream position that deserves a hearing. Kurzgesagt's popular 2018 explainer ["A Selfish Argument for Making the World a Better Place"](/e/selfish-argument-kurzgesagt) argues we live in a [positive-sum world](/e/positive-sum-economics): since the Industrial Revolution growth has turned a fixed pie into a growing one, so it is in your narrow self-interest that everyone else prospers. Lift billions into a position to invent and to buy inventions, and you multiply the research aimed at whatever will kill you. It is a powerful argument for global development, and it shares the bundle's assumption that growth can continue indefinitely on a finite planet, which is exactly what the critics dispute. And on the question of who funds which stories: Kurzgesagt discloses that a number of its videos have been funded by the Gates Foundation.

> Doomers say: stop, or we all die. Accelerationists say: go faster, or we all lose. Both treat AGI as the hinge of history, and both expect a small group of technologists to steer it. If you disagree with *both*, what is the third option, and who would you have to persuade to make it real?

## Part VIII: From Philosophy to Laboratory

<div class="section-image my-8 flex justify-center"><img src="/future-of-ai/images/sections/web/section-8.jpg" alt="" loading="lazy" decoding="async" width="256" height="256" class="w-64 h-64 rounded-lg object-cover border border-surface-2"></div>

By the late 2010s the ideas had become institutions, and the institutions had become laboratories.

[DeepMind](/e/deepmind), founded in London in 2010 by Hassabis, [Shane Legg](/e/shane-legg) and [Mustafa Suleyman](/e/mustafa-suleyman) out of the Singularity Summit network, set out to "solve intelligence". Google bought it in 2014.

[OpenAI](/e/openai) was founded as a nonprofit in December 2015 by Altman, Musk, [Greg Brockman](/e/greg-brockman), [Ilya Sutskever](/e/ilya-sutskever) and others, to make sure AGI "benefits all of humanity" rather than one company. Its founders' fears came from Bostrom and Yudkowsky. In 2017 [Open Philanthropy](/e/open-philanthropy) gave it $30 million, and with the grant came a board seat for Holden Karnofsky. Then the mission met the money: a capped-profit arm, and eventually some $13 billion from [Microsoft](/e/microsoft).

### Anthropic

San Francisco, 2021. A group of researchers walks out of OpenAI, led by [Dario Amodei](/e/dario-amodei), for nearly five years its VP of Research, and his sister [Daniela Amodei](/e/daniela-amodei). Seven co-founders in all. Their company is [Anthropic](/e/anthropic), and the reason for the split is that with Microsoft's money arriving they believed OpenAI was tilting from safety toward commercialization.

Their answer was not to stop building but to build differently, as a public benefit corporation, on the argument that a safety-focused lab must be at the frontier to influence it. Critics call that the race in better manners. Its early funding included the **$500 million from FTX**, which the bankruptcy later traced to misused customer money: the fortune of the man who did most to damage effective altruism had helped finance one of its most important AI projects. Billions from [Amazon](/e/amazon) and [Google](/e/google) followed.

The network tightened further. Holden Karnofsky, GiveWell co-founder, Open Philanthropy leader and one of the people warned about Bankman-Fried in 2018, joined Anthropic in January 2025, where his wife, Daniela Amodei, is president. Nothing improper follows from this. But a map of who funds, governs, marries, warns and hires whom in this world is a very small, very dense graph, and that density is itself one of the critics' central claims. (You can explore that graph on this site: [the network map](/future-of-ai/graph).)

### What "AI safety" came to mean

["AI safety"](/e/ai-safety) sounds entirely innocent. Who wants unsafe AI? But in the EA context it took on a narrow meaning: preventing **hypothetical future catastrophe**, the scenario in which an uncontrolled AI destroys or disempowers humanity. It did not mean discrimination, surveillance, labor or environmental cost. The field's vocabulary encodes the split: **AI safety** (alignment, existential risk) versus [AI ethics](/e/ai-ethics) (bias, exploitation, power). EA-linked funders poured hundreds of millions into the first; researchers on the second, like Gebru's [DAIR](/e/dair) and the [AI Now Institute](/e/ai-now-institute), ran on a fraction of it. The labor underneath "safe" AI was part of the second story: in 2023 *TIME* reported that Kenyan workers at the contractor [Sama](/e/sama) labeled horrific content for OpenAI for under $2 an hour, the work that made the chatbot safe to talk to. [80,000 Hours](/e/eighty-thousand-hours), EA's career adviser, has ranked AI risk as the world's most pressing problem since 2016; climate change sits among "other pressing problems", judged not neglected enough on the framework's own criteria.

And yet EA's influence on AI safety cannot be denied. OpenAI grew out of this community's concerns; Anthropic was founded by people close to it; the safety teams at every major lab are full of researchers shaped by it; and today's government tests for AI-enabled bioweapons and cyberattacks build on their work. **The first people to take the risks of AI seriously were members of this community.** However justified the criticisms, that historical fact does not go away.

## Part IX: Who Decides?

<div class="section-image my-8 flex justify-center"><img src="/future-of-ai/images/sections/web/section-9.jpg" alt="" loading="lazy" decoding="async" width="256" height="256" class="w-64 h-64 rounded-lg object-cover border border-surface-2"></div>

San Francisco, Friday 17 November 2023. OpenAI's nonprofit board, whose legal duty runs to the mission and not to investors, removes CEO **Sam Altman** for not being "consistently candid in his communications". Four of its six members vote him out: Sutskever, [Adam D'Angelo](/e/adam-dangelo), and two members seen as close to EA and AI-safety circles, [Helen Toner](/e/helen-toner) and [Tasha McCauley](/e/tasha-mccauley). Reporting describes a split over speed versus safety.

What followed took five days. Microsoft offered to hire Altman and anyone who came with him. **745 of OpenAI's 770 employees** signed a letter threatening to leave unless the board resigned; Sutskever signed it and apologized. On 22 November Altman returned, and a new board under [Bret Taylor](/e/bret-taylor) replaced the members who had removed him ([the crisis](/e/openai-board-crisis)).

For effective altruism it was a second catastrophe a year after FTX. But the deeper lesson was not about EA. The board had done exactly what it was designed to do, and it lost to the employees whose equity was at stake and the investor whose billions were. The mechanism meant to put safety above profit could be used once, and when it was used, it failed.

> Whoever you think was right in November 2023, answer this: who was *actually* in charge of OpenAI that week? Now ask who is in charge of the most powerful AI systems today. Could you name the person? Could you remove them? Could anyone?

Five answers to that question are currently on offer.

### 1. The labs: rules you write yourself

The first is [industry self-governance](/e/industry-self-governance). Anthropic created a [Long-Term Benefit Trust](/e/long-term-benefit-trust) in 2023: five financially disinterested trustees, several with EA ties, holding special shares that let them elect a growing part of the board. It published a [Responsible Scaling Policy](/e/responsible-scaling-policy) committing it to stronger safeguards, or a pause, as its models cross danger thresholds; rival labs followed with their own frameworks, and at the 2024 Seoul summit the frontier labs signed voluntary safety commitments.

These are serious inventions made by serious people. They share one weakness: the company writes, interprets and can revise its own rules. When OpenAI's head of alignment, [Jan Leike](/e/jan-leike), resigned in May 2024, he wrote that "safety culture and processes have taken a backseat to shiny products." In October 2025 OpenAI completed its [recapitalization](/e/openai-recapitalization) into a public benefit corporation, its nonprofit keeping control and about 26% against Microsoft's 27%. The first version of the plan would have ended nonprofit oversight altogether, and was revised only after the attorneys general of Delaware and California intervened. The fate of a mission to benefit "all of humanity" was negotiated, in the end, between a company, its investors and two state officials.

### 2. The funders: agendas you pay for

The second answer is quieter: whoever pays for the research decides which questions get asked. For a decade the largest single funder of AI safety was Open Philanthropy, which is to say, largely, the priorities of one couple. Its $30 million to OpenAI in 2017 came with a board seat. In 2019 it gave **$55 million** to launch Georgetown's [Center for Security and Emerging Technology](/e/cset), led by the former intelligence-research chief [Jason Matheny](/e/jason-matheny), with Helen Toner as director of strategy. CSET became one of Washington's main sources of expertise on AI, chips and China. EA-linked money did not just build a field. It helped build the vocabulary governments would use, and that vocabulary was increasingly about **security**.

### 3. The states: from safety to security

The third answer is the state, and here the story turns.

In November 2023, at **Bletchley Park**, 28 countries and the EU signed a declaration on frontier-AI risk, and Britain and the United States announced AI *Safety* Institutes. It was the high-water mark of AI safety as an international project ([the summits](/e/ai-safety-summits)).

Fifteen months later, at the [Paris AI Action Summit](/e/paris-ai-action-summit) in February 2025, sixty signatories backed a declaration on "inclusive and sustainable" AI; the **United States and Britain refused to sign**, and the US vice president warned that too much regulation could kill the industry. Three days later Britain renamed its institute the [AI Security Institute](/e/uk-ai-security-institute). In June the US Commerce Secretary turned the US AI Safety Institute into the [Center for AI Standards and Innovation](/e/us-ai-safety-institute), declaring that "innovators will no longer be limited by these standards." In July the White House published its AI plan under a title that could serve as this chapter's epigraph: [Winning the Race](/e/ai-action-plan-2025).

[AI security](/e/ai-security) is not a cynical rebranding. Bioweapons and cyberattacks are real, and governments taking them seriously is progress. But the one-word change moved the question to a new owner. "Safety" had been the lab's value and the researcher's vocabulary. "Security" is the state's prerogative, and it tends to move decisions toward defense budgets and classified briefings, the least democratic corners of government.

Elsewhere, lawmakers tried to write rules in public. The [EU AI Act](/e/eu-ai-act), the world's first comprehensive AI law, has placed duties on general-purpose models since August 2025, though parts were delayed in 2026 under pressure over competitiveness. California's governor [vetoed](/e/sb-1047-veto) a tough frontier-AI bill in 2024, then in September 2025 signed a lighter one, [SB 53](/e/sb-53). Three months later a US [executive order](/e/state-preemption-order) set up a Justice Department task force to challenge state AI laws in the name of "global AI dominance". Who governs frontier AI in America is itself now contested.

In February 2026, [New Delhi](/e/india-ai-impact-summit) hosted the first summit in the series held in the Global South. Its declaration, endorsed by 92 countries and organizations, spoke of "welfare for all". Read the summit names in order (Safety, Action, Impact) and you can watch the question of who AI is *for* change hands.

### 4. The courts: when safety meets security

The fourth answer arrived in 2026, and it turned the abstract debate into a lawsuit.

Anthropic had written two limits into its terms: its models should not be used for fully autonomous weapons or for domestic mass surveillance. The Pentagon wanted access for "all lawful purposes". On 27 February 2026 the President ordered federal agencies to stop using Anthropic's technology. On 5 March the [Department of Defense](/e/us-department-of-defense) designated the company a supply-chain risk to national security, a label normally reserved for foreign adversaries and never before applied to an American firm. On 27 August a federal judge ruled the designation "unlawful retaliation" and "arbitrary and capricious". The case continues ([the dispute](/e/anthropic-pentagon-dispute)).

Look at the structure rather than the parties. A private company's safety policy, which nobody elected, collided with a government's security claim, which no court had reviewed, and a judge had to decide between them. You can believe the company was right, or the Pentagon was, and still see the gap: there was no democratic rule to apply, only a contest of powers.

### 5. The missing

The fifth answer is the one almost nobody has offered: the people affected.

In none of these structures (the boards, the trusts, the scaling policies, the foundations, the institutes, the summits) do the Kenyan data labelers have a seat, or the artists whose work trained the models, or the communities whose water cools the data centres, or the billions of people outside the countries racing to build AGI. Anthony Kalulu's complaint about charity, that the solutions were decided for the poor and not with them, turns out to have been an early warning about AI governance. Gebru's point about AI ethics was the same point: the harms fall on people with no say.

This is what the TESCREAL critique, at its best, is really about. Not whether any one person believes seven things, but that a very small, very dense network of people, sharing a worldview that treats AI as the hinge of history, has so far answered the question *who decides?* with *us*.

## Part X: Oxford, 2026

<div class="section-image my-8 flex justify-center"><img src="/future-of-ai/images/sections/web/section-10.jpg" alt="" loading="lazy" decoding="async" width="256" height="256" class="w-64 h-64 rounded-lg object-cover border border-surface-2"></div>

Walk Oxford's stone courtyards today and the name once whispered in its corridors is spoken more carefully.

In March 2025 the CEO of the Centre for Effective Altruism, [Zach Robinson](/e/zach-robinson), wrote plainly that "EA has been in a defensive crouch for much of the past two-plus years." It had "defied predictions that the collapse of FTX would cause the collapse of EA, but our momentum stalled." By early 2026 engagement was growing again. The movement never lost its money, as the headlines predicted: Moskovitz and Tuna kept funding, which left it more dependent on one couple than before. What it lost was innocence. MacAskill now warns against assuming that people who display strong moral commitment, giving a lot or going vegan, must have integrity in everything else.

Other chapters closed. In April 2024 Oxford [closed](/e/fhi-closure) the Future of Humanity Institute after nineteen years. In January 2023 Bostrom had [apologized](/e/bostrom-email-apology) for a 1996 email to the Extropians mailing list claiming that "Blacks are more stupid than whites", and using a slur. He repudiated it, and said he had apologized within a day at the time. For critics, the apology confirmed rather than dispelled the worry about the genealogy. But the institute's people had long since moved into the places where AI is decided: the labs, the funders, the security institutes.

### The ledger

The legacy is too complicated for a sentence.

On one side, children alive today because of bed nets. On the other, the victims of an $8 billion fraud.

On one side, researchers who took the risks of AI seriously before anyone else. On the other, a definition of "safety" that for years left out the people already being harmed.

On one side, many thousands of ordinary people who pledged 10% of their income and kept the promise. On the other, a funding structure with no democratic oversight that handed the agenda to a few billionaires.

On one side, labs that invented new ways to bind themselves to a mission. On the other, a race in which every binding has so far been loosened by money, rivals or the state.

### What to build instead

The critics are not only critics. [Ruha Benjamin](/e/ruha-benjamin), the sociologist of race, justice and technology, observes that a small sliver of humanity is imposing its visions on the rest of us, investing in space travel, superintelligence and bunkers while casting healthcare and housing for all as unimaginable. Their imaginations run wild about bending physical and digital reality, she says, but "grow limp" when it comes to transforming social reality. Utopia and apocalypse are two sides of one coin: in both, technology acts *on* us and the human inputs go missing. "To move forward, we have to pull back the screen." Borrowing a word from [Margaret Atwood](/e/margaret-atwood), she proposes [ustopia](/e/ustopia): "Utopias are the stuff of dreams, dystopias the stuff of nightmares; ustopias are what we create together when we're wide awake."

There are working versions. [Decidim](/e/decidim), the free, open-source participatory-democracy platform, is used by Barcelona, Helsinki and New York. DAIR's series *Possible Futures* imagines technology grown from community needs: [Nyalleng Moorosi](/e/nyalleng-moorosi) describes tools that strengthen existing informal food-trading networks, "working within familiar cultural frameworks rather than imposed foreign concepts of commerce." The EU's AI Act and California's SB 53 are imperfect, but they are rules made in public by people who can be voted out. Even inside the bundle, Buterin's d/acc asks which technologies spread power and which concentrate it.

Why is all this so much harder to picture than a Mars colony? The activist [Rob Hopkins](/e/rob-hopkins) calls it a [crisis of imagination](/e/crisis-of-imagination): "Given everything that humanity has accomplished, all of it driven by leaps of imagination, why is envisioning a safer, saner, more peaceful path forward so consistently beyond our reach?"

And against the technologist's clean slate — Levandowski: "in technology, all that matters is tomorrow" — there is [James Baldwin](/e/james-baldwin) in [The Fire Next Time](/e/the-fire-next-time): *"To accept one's past, one's history, is not the same thing as drowning in it; it is learning how to use it. An invented past can never be used; it cracks and crumbles under the pressures of life like clay in a season of extreme drought."*

### The question

Fifty-five years ago, in this same city, a young philosopher asked whether you would wade into a pond.

The three questions hidden in "do the most good" have followed us all the way here. *Good for whom?* The movement answered: everyone, including the unborn. *Over what horizon?* Millions of years. The third it never answered, and the question has now outgrown it. It belongs to everyone who will live with what the laboratories build.

The arithmetic was always the easy part. The hard part is the thing the arithmetic quietly decides on your behalf: *whose* child, *which* future, and *who gets to hold the calculator*.

> **Will you see the drowning child? And when the decisions about the most powerful technology of our time are being made, will you be in the room, or in the pond?**
